Show that $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is a closed subspace of $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$, and deduce using Lemma 4.3 that $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is complete.

**Step 1: $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is a subspace of $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$**
WTS $c_0(\mathbb{K}) \subset l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$.
If $x_j \rightarrow 0$ then the sequence is bounded. Hence $c_0(\mathbb{K}) \subset l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$.
WTS closed under addition.
If $x,y \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$ then $x_j \rightarrow 0$ and $y_j \rightarrow 0$, so $(x_j + y_j) \rightarrow 0$.
Thus, $x+y \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$.
WTS closed under scalar multiplication.
If $\lambda \in \mathbb{K}$ and $x \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$, then $(\lambda x_j) \rightarrow 0$, so $\lambda x \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$.
Therefore, $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is a linear subspace of $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$.

**Step 2: $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is closed in $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$**
Let $(x^{(n)}) \subset c_0(\mathbb{K})$ and suppose $x^{(n)} \rightarrow x \in l^{\infty} (\mathbb{K})$.
WTS $x \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$ ie $x_j \rightarrow 0$.
Fix $\epsilon > 0$.
Because $x^{(n)} \rightarrow x$ in the sup norm, there exists $N$ s.t. $\Vert x^{(n)} - x \Vert_{\infty} < \epsilon/2$ for all $n \ge N$.
Fix such an $n$. Since $x^{(n)} \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$, there exists $J$ s.t. $|x_j^{(n)}| < \epsilon/2$ for all $j \ge J$.
Then for all $j \ge J$, $| x_j | \le | x_j - x_j^{(n)}| + |x_j^{(n)}| < \epsilon/2 + \epsilon/2 = \epsilon$
Thus, $x_j \rightarrow 0$, so $x \in c_0(\mathbb{K})$.
Therefore, $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is closed in $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$.

**Step 3: deduce using Lemma 4.3 that $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is complete**
Per Theorem 4.7, $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$ is complete.
$c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is a closed linear subspace of $l^{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$.
Therefore, by Lemma 4.3, $c_0(\mathbb{K})$ is complete.

- A finite-dimensional property is that a finite supremum behaves like a finite maximum, and maxima commute with limits.
