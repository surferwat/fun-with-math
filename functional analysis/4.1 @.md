Show that in a normed space $(X, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ $(i)$ any Cauchy sequence is bounded; and $(ii)$ if $(x_n)$ is Cauchy and $x_{n_k} \to x$ for some subsequence $(x_{n_k})$, then $x_n \to x$.

$\mathit{Proof}$ for $(i)$
Let $(x_n) \in X$ be a Cauchy sequence
By definition of Cauchy, let $\epsilon = 1$.
Then there exists an index $N$ s.t. $\Vert x_n - x_m \Vert < 1$ for all $m,n \ge N$.
Fix this $N$. Let $x_N$ be the reference point and consider any $n \ge N$.
Then $\Vert x_n \Vert = \Vert x_n - X_N + X_N \Vert \le \Vert x_n - x_N \Vert + \Vert x_N \Vert < 1 + \Vert x_N \Vert$. 
Thus all, $x_n$ with $n \ge N$ lie in the ball of radius $1+\Vert x_N \Vert$.
The finitely many points $x1, ..., x_{n-1}$ are also bounded (finite sets are always bounded).
Therefore, the $(x_n)$ is bounded. $\blacksquare$

$\mathit{Proof}$ for $(ii)$
Let $\epsilon > 0$.
Because $x_{n_k} \rightarrow x$, there exists $K$ s.t. $\Vert x_{n_k} - x \Vert < \epsilon/2$ for all $k \ge K$.
Because $(x_n)$ is Cauchy, there exists $N$ s.t. $\Vert x_n - x_m \Vert < \epsilon/2$ for all $m,n \ge N$.
Choose $k$ large enough so that $n_k \ge N$ and $k \ge K$.
Then for any $n \ge N$, using Minkowski's Inequality, we have $\Vert x_n - x_{n_k} \Vert + \Vert x_{n_k} - x \Vert \ge \Vert ( x_n - x_{n_k})+ (x_{n_k} - x) \Vert = \Vert x_n - x \Vert$.
Thus, for all $n \ge N$, $\Vert x_n - x \Vert \le \Vert x_n - x_{n_k} \Vert + \Vert x_{n_k} - x \Vert < \epsilon/2 + \epsilon/2 = \epsilon \Rightarrow \Vert x_n - x \Vert < \epsilon \Rightarrow x_n \rightarrow x$.

Notes
- The fact that every Cauchy sequence is bounded is a fundamental property used in the study of Banach spaces (complete normed spaces) since (1) if Cauchy sequences were unbounded, limits could not exist and (2) boundedness ensures that potential limits live in a controlled region of the space.
- The convergence of a subsequence forces the whole Cauchy sequence to converge - this matters since (1) it shows that Cauchy sequences cannot "wander off" once part of them settles toward a limit, (2) it is used frequently in functional analysis to prove convergence of sequences of functions, operators, etc., and (3) it links two essential ideas: Cauchy behavior (internal consistency) and subsequence convergence (external convergence).
- Normed spaces behave in an orderly, predictable way (i.e., Cauchy sequences are bounded, converge is "unique" in the sense that a Cauchy sequence cannot have two distinct subsequential limits) similar to familiar finite-dimensional spaces (e.g., $\mathbb{R}$).
