Show that the space $C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ is complete when equipped with the metric $d(f,g) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}\frac{\Vert f -g \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1+\Vert f-g \Vert_{[-n,n]}}$ where $\Vert f \Vert_{[-n,n]} := max_{x \in [-n,n]} | f(x) |$. Show that this metric does correspond to a norm. (Goffman and Pedrick, 1983)

Let $C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ be the space of continuous real-valued functions on $\mathbb{R}$. 
For $n \in \mathbb{N}$, define the semi-norm $\Vert f \Vert_{[-n,n]} := max_{x\in[-n,n]} | f(x) |$. 
Define the metric $d(f,g) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} \frac{\Vert f - g \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1 + \Vert f - g \Vert_{[-n,n]}}$.

**Step 1: Use the definition of what it means for a sequence to be Cauchy to identify a possible limit**
Let $(f_k)$ be a Cauchy sequence in $(C(\mathbb{R}), d)$.

Fix $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $\epsilon > 0$. Note that $\frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon} > 0$. 
Since $(f_k)$ is Cauchy in $d$, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $d(f_k, f_m) < \frac{1}{2^n} \frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}$ for $k, m \ge N$. 
Then for $k,m \ge N$, $\frac{ \Vert f_k - f_m \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1 + \Vert f_k - f_m \Vert_{[-n,n]}} \le 2^n d(f_k, f_m) < \frac{\epsilon}{1+\epsilon}$.
Since the function $t \mapsto \frac{t}{1+t}$ is strictly increasing on $[0, \infty)$, this implies $\Vert f_k - f_m \Vert_{[-n,n]} < \epsilon$. 
Thus, $\Vert f_k - f_m \Vert_{[-n,n]} \to 0$ as $k,m \to \infty$.

Hence, for each fixed $n$, the sequence $(f_k)$ is uniformly Cauchy on $[-n,n]$. 
Since $C([-n,n])$ is complete with respect to the sup norm, there exists a continuous function $f^{(n)} : [-n,n] \to \mathbb{R}$ s.t. $f_k \to f^{(n)}$ as $k \to \infty$ uniformly on $[-n,n]$.
If $m > n$, then uniform convergence on $[-m,m]$ implies uniform convergence on $[-n,n]$ so $f^{(m)}|_{[-n,n]} = f^{(n)}$. 
Hence the family $\{ f^{(n)} \}$ is compatible, and we may define a function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ by $f(x) := f^{(n)}(x)$ whenever $|x| \le n$.
This definition is independent of the choice of $n$, so $f$ is well defined.

**Step 2: Show that the original sequence converges to this "possible limit" in the appropriate norm**
Fix $\epsilon > 0$.
Choose $N$ so that $\sum_{n = N+1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.
For each $1 \le n \le N$, we know that $\Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]} \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$.
Hence, for each $n$, there exists $K_n$ s.t. for all $k \ge K_n$, $\frac{\Vert f_k -f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1+ \Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}} < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.
Let $K = max\{K_1,..., K_N\}$.
Then for all $k \ge K$, $\sum_{n=1}^N \frac{1}{2^n} \frac{\Vert f_k -f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1 + \Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}} \le \sum_{n = 1}^{N} \frac{1}{2^n} \cdot \frac{\epsilon}{2} < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.
For $k \ge K$, $d(f_k,f) = \sum_{n=1}^N \frac{1}{2^n} \frac{\Vert f_k -f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1 + \Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}} + \sum_{n = N + 1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} < \frac{\epsilon}{2} + \frac{\epsilon}{2} = \epsilon$.
Thus, $d(f_k,f) \to 0$ as $k \to \infty$.

**Step 3: Check that the "limit" lies in the correct space**
Fix $x \in \mathbb{R}$.
Choose $n \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $x \in [-n,n]$.
On the interval $[-n,n]$ we have $f_k \to f$ uniformly, since $f = f^{(n)}$ on $[-n,n]$.
Each $f_k$ is continuous on $[-n,n]$ and the uniform limit of continuous functions on a compact interval is continuous. 
Hence $f$ is continuous at $x$.
Since $x$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
Therefore, $f \in C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$.

Conclusion
Every Cauchy sequence in $(C(\mathbb{R}), d)$ converges to an element of $C(\mathbb{R})$. 
$(C(\mathbb{R}), d)$ is complete.

Notes:
- Definition 4.1 A normed space $(X, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ is complete if every Cauchy sequence in $x$ converges in $X$ (to a limit that lies in $X$). A Banach space is a complete normed space. 
- Let $(x_n)$ be a sequence in $C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$. If it converges, then it must be Cauchy. Then, for any $\epsilon > 0$, there exists an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $d(x_n,x_m) < \epsilon$ for all $n,m \ge N$.
- Each term in $d(f_k,f)$ has two components, fast decaying part: $\frac{1}{2^n}$ and less than or equal to 1 part: $\frac{\Vert f_k -  f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1 + \Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}$. Two facts should jump out immediately about the expression, 1) each term is bounded by $\frac{1}{2^n}$ and 2) $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}$ converges. Whenever you see an infinite series and no uniform control over all terms, the standard move in analysis is: control finitely many terms using convergence, and control the tail using the series itself. In regards to the latter, since $0 \le \frac{\Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1+\Vert f_k - f \Vert_[-n,n]} \le 1$ we always have $\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n} \frac{\Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}}{1+ \Vert f_k - f \Vert_{[-n,n]}} \le \sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2^n}$. And the right-hand side depends only on $N$, goes to $0$ as $N \to \infty$.
