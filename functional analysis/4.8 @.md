Suppose that $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is Lipschitz continuous, i.e., satisfies $| f(x) - f(y) | \le L|x-y|$, $x,y \in \mathbb{R}$, for some $L > 0$. Use the Contraction Mapping Theorem in the space $C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$ on the mapping $(\delta x)(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$ to show that (4.10) has a unique solution on any time interval $[0, T]$ with $LT < 1$. By considering the solution to (4.10) starting at $x(T)$ deduce that in fact (4.10) has a unique solution that exists for all $t \ge 0$.

Let $f : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be Lipschitz continuous: $| f(x) - f(y) | \le L|x-y|$ $\forall x,y \in \mathbb{R}$. 
Consider the initial value problem $x'(t) = f(x(t))$ and $x(0) = x_0$. 
Show that (4.10) has a unique solution on any interval $[0,T]$ with $LT < 1$, and deduce that the solution exists uniquely for all $t \ge 0$. 

**Step 1: Functional setting and completeness**
Let $X := C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$ equipped with the supremum norm $\Vert x \Vert_{\infty} = sup_{t \in [0,T]} |x(T)|$. 
Since $[0,T]$ is compact, every continuous function is bounded, so $C([0,T]; \mathbb{R}) = C_b([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$. 
By 4.10, this space is complete. Hence $X$ is a Banach space.

**Step 2: Define the Picard operator**
Define the operator $\delta : X \rightarrow X$ by $(\delta x)(t) := x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$.
If $x \in X$ then $f \, \circ \, x$ is continuous (since $f$ is Lipschitz) so $\delta x \in X$.

**Step 3: $\delta$ is a contraction when $LT < 1$.**
Let $x,y \in X$. For any $t \in [0,T]$, $| (\delta x) (t) - (\delta y)(t) |$
$= | \int_0^t (f(x(s)) - f(y(s))) \, ds |$
$\le \int_0^t |f(x(s)) - f(y(s)) | ds$
$\le \int_0^t L|x(s)-y(s)|ds$
$\le \int_0^t L\Vert x-y\Vert_{\infty}ds = L\Vert x-y\Vert_{\infty}\int_0^t ds = Lt \Vert x-y\Vert_{\infty}$
$\le LT \Vert x -y \Vert_{\infty}$.
Taking the supremum over $t \in [0,T], \Vert \delta x - \delta y \Vert_{\infty} \le LT \Vert x - y \Vert_{\infty}$.
Thus, if $LT < 1$, $\delta$ is a contraction on $X$. 

**Step 4. Local existence and uniqueness**
By the Contraction Mapping Theorem, $\delta$ has a unique fixed point $x \in X$.
$(\delta x)(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$.
Since $x$ is a fixed point, $x(t) = (\delta x)(t)$.
This fixed point satisfies $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$, which is equivalent to solving 4.10 on $[0,T]$. 
Hence 4.10 has a unique solution on $[0,T]$ whenever $LT < 1$.

**Step 5. Extension to all $t \ge 0$**
Let $T > 0$ satisfy $LT < 1$ and let $x(t)$ be the unique solution on $[0,T]$. 
Consider the problem $y'(t) = f(y(t))$ and $y(T) = x(T)$.
Repeating the same contraction argument yields a unique solution on $[T, 2T]$.
By uniqueness, this extension agrees with the original solution on $[0,T]$.
Iterating this procedure produces a unique solution on $[0, nT]$ for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$. Therefore, the solution exists and is unique on $[0, \infty)$.

**Final conclusion**
By 4.10, $C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$ is complete. 
The Picard operator is a contraction when $LT < 1$.
This gives local existence and uniqueness.
Repeated continuation yields a unique global solution for all $t \ge 0$.

Notes
- Lipschitz continuous means the graph of f has a **global bound on its slope**. No matter where you zoom in, the function doesn’t get “too steep”. Think: _no sudden sharp spikes_. Geometrically, the graph always lies between two cones of slope ±L.
- The usual Contraction Mapping Theorem requires a uniform constant $0 < \kappa < 1$ s.t. $\Vert f(x) - f(y) \Vert \le \kappa \Vert x - y \Vert$ $\forall x,y \in K$. 
- "Use the Contraction Mapping Theorem in the space  $C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$ on the mapping $(\delta x)(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$ " means use the Contraction Mapping Theorem on $t \mapsto \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$.
