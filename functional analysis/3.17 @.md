
Show that a normed space $(X, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ is separable if and only if there exists a compact set $K \subset X$ s.t $X = clin(K)$.

$\mathit{Proof}$
($\Rightarrow$)
Assume $X$ is separable.
Then there exists a countable dense subset $D = \{x_1, x_2, x_3, ... \} \subset X$.
Define $K := \{ \frac{x_n}{n(1+\Vert x_n \Vert)}:n \in \mathbb{N}, x_n \ne 0 \} \cup \{ 0 \}$.
WTS: $K$ is compact.
The sequence $\frac{x_n}{n}$ tends to $0$ because $\Vert \frac{x_n}{n(1+\Vert x_n \Vert)} \Vert = \frac{\Vert x_n \Vert}{n(1+\Vert x_n \Vert)} \le \frac{1}{n}$ since $\frac{\Vert x_n \Vert}{1+\Vert x_n \Vert} \le 1$. 
The norms of the non-zero elements in $K$ are bounded by $\frac{1}{n}$ for their corresponding index $n$, and as $n \to \infty$, these elements tend to $0$ in norm.
Thus, the set $K$ is compact since the union of $\{ 0 \}$ and a sequence converging to $0$, which means that any infinite subsequence must eventually consist of terms from the sequence tending to $0$ so it to converges to $0$.

Let $x_k \in D$ with $x_k \ne 0$. 
$x_k = k(1+\Vert x_k \Vert) \cdot (\frac{x_k}{k(1+\Vert x_k \Vert)}) \implies x_k \in Span(K)$.
If $x_k = 0$ it is trivially in $Span(K)$
Hence $D \subset Span(K)$.
Since $D$ is dense in $X$, it follows that $Span(K)$ is dense in $X$. 
Thus, $clin(K) = X$.

($\Leftarrow$)
Compact sets in metric spaces are separable.
Since $(X, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ is a normed space, it is a metric space, thus, $K$ is separable. 
So, $K$ contains a countable dense subset $D = \{ x_1, x_2, x_3, ... \} \subset K$.

Consider $M$, a set of linear combinations with rational coefficients: $M = \{ \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i x_{k_i} : q_i \in \mathbb{Q}, n, k_i \in \mathbb{N} \}$.
Since $D$ is countable and $\mathbb{Q}$ is countable, the set $M$ is a union of countable sets, thus, $M$ is countable.

Since $D \subset X$, $\exists x \in Span(D)$.
Let $x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i x_{k_i}$.
Since $\mathbb{Q}$ is dense in $\mathbb{R}$, we can approximate each $r_i$ by $q_i \in \mathbb{Q}$ s.t. $| r_i - q_i | < \epsilon/(n \cdot max_j \Vert x_j \Vert)$.
Then $\Vert \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_i x_{k_i} - \sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i x_{k_i} \Vert \le \sum_{i=1}^{n} | r_i - q_i | \Vert x_{k_i} \Vert < \epsilon$.
Thus, $x$ can be approximated arbitrarily closely by elements of $M$.
Thus, $M$ is dense in $Span(D)$.

By assumption, $X = clin(K)$.
Since $D \subset K$ is dense in $K$, we have $clin(D) = clin(K) = X$.
And since $M$ is dense in $span(D)$, $\overline{M} = clin(D) = X$.
$M$ is countable and dense in $X$.
Therefore, $X$ is separable.

Notes
- Why $D$ may not be linear: $D$ is just a dense subset of points in $K$. There is no guarantee that it is closed under addition or scalar multiplication. So, though $M$ is dense in $D$, closure of $M$ only gives us $\overline{M} = D$. Where as closure of $D$ gives us $clin(D) = clin(K)$.
