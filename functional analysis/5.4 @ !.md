Show that if $Y$ is a finite-dimensional subspace of a normed space $X$ and $x\in X$ \ $Y$, then there exists $y\in Y$ with $\Vert x-y \Vert= dist(x,Y)$. 

$\mathit{proof}$
Define $d := dist(x,Y) = inf_{y \in Y} \Vert x - y \Vert$.
By definition of the infimum, there exists a sequence $(y_n) \subset Y$ s.t. $\Vert x - y_n \Vert \to d$.
The sequence $(y_n)$ is bounded. Indeed, since $\Vert x - y_n \Vert$ converges, there exists $M > 0$ s.t. $\Vert x - y_n \Vert \le M$ for all $n$. 
Then $\Vert y_n \Vert \le \Vert x \Vert + \Vert x - y_n \Vert \le \Vert x \Vert + M$, so $(y_n)$ is bounded in $Y$.
Because $Y$ is finite-dimensional, every bounded sequence in $Y$ has a convergent subsequence. Hence there exists a subsequence $(y_{n_k})$ and some $y \in Y$ s.t. $y_{n_k} \to y$.
By continuity of the norm, $\Vert x -y \Vert = lim_{k \to \infty} \Vert x - y_{n_k} \Vert = d$.
Therefore, there exists $y \in Y$ s.t. $\Vert x - y \Vert = dist(x,Y)$, as claimed. $\blacksquare$.

Notes
- In $\mathbb{R}^2$ think of $x =$ a point and $Y =$ a line. The distance from $x$ to the line is the length of the shortest perpendicular segment. That length is exactly the smallest possible value of $\Vert x - y \Vert$ as $y$ ranges over the line. The infimum generalizes this idea to arbitrary normed spaces.
- The norm map $\Vert \cdot \Vert : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous. Concretely, this means: if $z_n \to z$ in $X$ then $\Vert z_n \Vert \to \Vert z \Vert$. This follows from the reverse triangle inequality: $| \Vert z_n \Vert - \Vert z \Vert | \le \Vert z_n - z \Vert$. 
- This problem helps to formalize "best approximation". The quantity $dist(x,Y) = inf_{y\in Y} \Vert x - y \Vert$ means "how well can elements of $Y$ approximate $x$?" This theorem says "If $Y$ is finite-dimensional" then $\mathit{best}$ $\mathit{approximation}$ $\mathit{actually}$ $\mathit{exist}$. This is the mathematical backbone of: least squares methods, projection techniques, numerical approximation in finite models.
