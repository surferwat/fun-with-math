Show that if $(f_n)$, $f \in C([0,1])$ and $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} \to 0$ (uniform convergence) then $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{L^p} \to 0$ (convergence in $L^p$) and $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x \in [0,1]$ ('pointwise convergence'). (For related results see Exercises 7.1-7.3).

$\mathit{Proof}$
$\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} = max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |$
$\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{L^p} = (\int_{0}^{1} | f_n-f |^p dx)^{\frac{1}{p}}$

WTS: $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{L^p} \to 0$ (convergence in $L^p$) for every $x \in [0,1]$.
Let $x_k \in [0,1]$ s.t. $| f_n(x_k) - f(x_k) | = max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |$
Then $| f_n - f | \le max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |$ for any $x \in [0,1]$.
$| f_n - f |^p \le max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |^p$.
$\int_{0}^{1} | f_n - f |^p dx \le \int_{0}^{1} max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |^p dx = max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |^p \int_{0}^{1} dx = max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |^p$
 $(\int_{0}^{1} | f_n - f |^p dx)^{\frac{1}{p}} \le (max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |^p)^{\frac{1}{p}}$
 $(\int_{0}^{1} | f_n - f |^p dx)^{\frac{1}{p}} \le max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |$
Since $max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |=\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty}$ and by assumption, $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} \to 0$ (uniform convergence), it follows that $max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f | < \epsilon$.
Thus, $(\int_{0}^{1} | f_n - f |^p dx)^{\frac{1}{p}} < \epsilon \implies \Vert f_n - f \Vert_{L^p} \to 0$ (convergence in $L^p$).

WTS: $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x \in [0,1]$ ('pointwise convergence').
Let $x_k \in [0,1]$ s.t. $| f_n(x_k) - f(x_k) | = max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |$
Then $| f_n - f | \le max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |$ for any $x \in [0,1]$.
Since $max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f |=\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty}$ and by assumption, $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} \to 0$ (uniform convergence), it follows that $max_{x\in[0,1]} | f_n - f | < \epsilon$.
Thus, $| f_n - f | < \epsilon \implies f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x \in [0,1]$ ('pointwise convergence'). $\blacksquare$

Alternate solution which is more simplistic and avoids unnecessary use of max $x \in [0,1]$.
WTS: $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x \in [0,1]$ ('pointwise convergence').
By assumption, $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} \to 0$ (uniform convergence).
By definition of uniform convergence:
For every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. for any $n \ge N$ and for all $x \in [0,1]$, $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} < \epsilon$.
Fix $y \in [0,1]$.
Since the $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} < \epsilon$ holds for all $x \in [0,1]$, it holds for $y \in [0,1]$.
Thus, $| f_n(y) - f(y) | < \epsilon$ for all $n \ge N$.
Since $y \in [0,1]$ was arbitrary, $f_n(x) \to f(x)$ for every $x \in [0,1]$ ('pointwise convergence').
 
 Notes
 - $\Vert f_n-f \Vert \to 0$ means that for every $\epsilon > 0$, there exists an $N \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. for any $n \ge N$ and for all $x \in [0,1]$, $\Vert f_n - f \Vert_{\infty} < \epsilon$.
 - There are different ways functions can "converge" to a limit: Pointwise convergence: convergence at each point; Uniform convergence: convergence uniformly across the whole domain; Convergence in $L^p$: convergence in an average sense, measured via integrals. These forms of convergence are not equivalent in general. This result shows: Uniform convergence is strong enough to imply both pointwise and $L^p$ convergence.
 - In approximation theory, analysis, and numerical methods, we often approximate a function $f$ with a sequence $f_n$​. This result reassures us: If the approximation is uniformly close, it's also close in terms of integrals ($L^p$ norms), and it captures the pointwise behavior as well.
