Show that any finite-dimensional subspace of a Banach space is closed.

**Step 1: Reduce to a sequence argument**
To prove that $M$ is closed, it suffices to show: if $(x_n) \subset M$ and $x_n \to x$ in $X$, then $x \in M$. 
So let $(x_n)$ be a convergent sequence in $M$, with $x_n \to x$ in $X$. 
We must prove $x \in M$.

**Step 2: Use the fact that norms are equivalent in finite dimensions**
Since $M$ is finite-dimensional, choose a basis: $e_1, ..., e_k$.
Then every $y \in M$ can be written uniquely as $y = \sum_{j=1}^k a_je_j$.
Define the coordinate map $T : \mathbb{R}^k \to M$, $T(a_1, ..., a_k) = \sum_{j=1}^k a_je_j$.
This is a linear bijection.
Now, since $M$ is finite-dimensional, all norms on $M$ are equivalent. In particular, there exists $C > 0$ s.t. for all scalars $a_1, ..., a_k$, $\Vert \sum_{j=1}^k a_j e_j \Vert \ge C \sqrt{a_1^2 + ... + a_k^2}$.
Equivalently, $\Vert (a_1, ..., a_k) \Vert_{\mathbb{R}^k} \le \frac{1}{C} \Vert \sum_{j=1}^k a_je_j \Vert$.
So the inverse map $T^{-1} : M \to \mathbb{R}^k$ is continuous (the coefficients $(a_1, ..., a_k)$ cannot blow up unless the vector in $M$ blows up, so convergence in $M$ forces convergence of coordinates in $\mathbb{R}^k$).

**Step 3: Apply this to the convergent sequence**
Write $x_n = \sum_{j=1}^k a_{n,j}e_j \in M$. 
Since $x_n \to x$ in $X$, the sequence $(x_n)$ is Cauchy. Thus $\Vert x_n - x_m \Vert \to 0$.
But then by the inequality above, $\Vert (a_{n,1} - a_{m,1}, ..., a_{n,k} - a_{m,k}) \Vert_{\mathbb{R}^k} \le \frac{1}{C} \Vert x_n - x_m \Vert$.
So the coefficient sequence $(a_{n,1}, ..., a_{n,k})$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}^k$. 
Since $\mathbb{R}^k$ is complete, there exists $(a_1, ..., a_k) \in \mathbb{R}^k$ such that $(a_{n,1}, ..., a_{n,k}) \to (a_1, ..., a_k)$.

**Step 4: Take the limit in $M$**
Define $y = \sum_{j=1}^k a_je_j \in M$. 
Then continuity of $T$, $x_n = T(a_{n,1}, ..., a_{n,k}) \to T(a_1, ..., a_k) = y$ in $M \subset X$.
But limits in a normed space are unique and $x_n \to x \in X$ by assumption, we must have $x=y \in M$.
Thus $M$ contains the limit of any convergent sequence in $M$.

**Conclusion**
Therefore, $M$ is closed in $X$.
Every finite-dimensional subspace of a Banach space is closed.

Robinson solution referenced

**Step 1: Pick a basis and define the coordinate map**
Let $\{ e_1, ..., e_k\}$ be a basis of $M$. 
Define the coordinate map $T : R^k \to M$, $T(a_1, ..., a_k) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} a_je_j$.
Since $M$ is finite-dimensional, all norms on $M$ are equivalent. Therefore, $T$ is a linear bijection and both $T$ and $T^{-1}$ are continuous.

**Step 2: Transfer completeness from $\mathbb{R}^k$ to $M$.**
$\mathbb{R}^k$ is complete.
Continuity of $T$ and $T^{-1}$ implies that $M$ is complete: any Cauchy sequence in $M$ corresponds under $T^{-1}$ to a Cauchy sequence in $\mathbb{R}^k$, which converges, and the image under $T$ gives a limit in $M$.
Thus $M$ is a complete normed space, i.e., a Banach space.

**Step 3: Apply the closed-subspace theorem**
Lemma 4.3 If $(X, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ is a Banach space and $Y$ is a linear subspace of $X$, then $(Y, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ is a Banach space if and only if $Y$ is closed.

**Conclusion**
Thus, it follows immediately that $M$ is closed in $X$.

Notes
- Finite-dimensional pieces of infinite-dimensional spaces behave like ordinary Euclidean space.
- The importance of this result lies in the fact that finite-dimensional subspaces of normed spaces inherit all essential topological properties without additional assumptions. In particular, no questions of completeness or closure arise in finite dimensions: every finite-dimensional subspace is automatically complete and therefore closed.
- Robinson-style solution prefers a clean conceptual route:
	- Finite-dimensional $\to$ coordinate map $T:\mathbb{R}^k \to M$.
	- Use equivalence of norms $\to T$ and $T^{-1}$ are continuous.
	- $\mathbb{R}^k$ complete $\to$ M complete.
	- Complete subspace of Banach space $\to M$ closed.
