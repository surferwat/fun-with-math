Show that $l_{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$ is complete.

A metric space in which any Cauchy sequence converges is called complete.
Cauchy means for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists an $N$ s.t. $d(x_n, x_m) < \epsilon$ for all $n,m \ge N$.
A normed space $(X, \Vert \cdot \Vert)$ is complete  if every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$ (to a limit that lies in $X$).

Identify a possible limit
Let $(x^{(n)})_{n\in \mathbb{N}} \in l_{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$ be a Cauchy sequence.
Each $x^{(n)}$ is itself a bounded sequence: $x^{(n)} = (x_1^{(n)}, x_2^{(n)}, x_3^{(n)},...)$.
The norm is $\Vert x^{(n)} - x^{(m)} \Vert_{l^\infty} = sup_{j \in \mathbb{N}} | x_j^{(n)} - x_j^{(m)}|$.

Since $x^{(n)}$ is Cauchy in $l_{\infty}$ for every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists $N$ s.t. $\Vert x^{(n)} - x^{(m)} \Vert_{\infty} < \epsilon$ $(n,m \ge N)$.
In particular, for each fixed coordinate $j$, $| x_j^{(n)} - x_j^{(m)} | \le \Vert x^{(n)} - x^{(m)} \Vert_{\infty} < \epsilon$.
Thus for each $j$, the sequence $(x_j^{(n)})_n$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{K}$, and since $\mathbb{K}$ is complete, it converges. Define $a_j := lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} x_j^{(n)}$.
Let $a=(a_1, a_2, a_3, ...)$.


Show that $a \in l_{\infty}(\mathbb{K})$.
Fix $\epsilon = 1$.
Since $(x^{(n)})$ is Cauchy, there exists $N$ s.t. $\Vert x^{(n)} - x^{(m)} \Vert_{\infty} < 1$ $(n,m \ge N)$.
Then for all $j$, $| x_j^{(n)} - x_j^{(N)} | < 1$ $(n \ge N)$.
Taking the limit as $n \rightarrow \infty$, $|a_j - x_j^{(N)}| \le 1$.
Thus, using the Minkowski inequality, $|a_j| \le |x_j^{(N)}| + 1$.
Taking sup over $j$: $sup \, |a_j | \le sup \, | x_j^{(N)} | + 1 = \Vert x^{(N)} \Vert_{\infty} + 1 < \infty$
Therefore, $a \in l_{\infty} (\mathbb{K})$
