Show that $c_0(K)$ is separable.

$\mathit{Proof}$
WTS: a countable dense subset of $c_0(K)$, proving it is separable.
Let $K = \mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$.
$c_0(K)$: the space of all sequences $x=(x_n) \in K$ s.t. $lim_{n \to \infty} x_n = 0$.
Equipped with the sup norm: $\Vert x \Vert_{\infty} = sup_n \Vert x_n \Vert$.

$A := \{(a_n) \in c_0(K) | a_n = 0$ for all but finitely many $n$, and each $a_n \in K_0$ } where $K_0 = \mathbb{Q}$ if $K = \mathbb{R}$ and $K_0=\mathbb{Q}+i\mathbb{Q}$.
$A$ is a subset of $c_0(K)$ since all sequences with finite support go to zero at infinity.
$A$ is countable since finite sequences over countable sets form a countable set.

Let $x=(x_n) \in K$ and $\epsilon > 0$.
Since $x_n \to 0$, there exists $N \in \mathbb{N}$ s.t. $| x_n | < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$ for all $n > N$.
For $n=1,...,N$, choose $a_n$ s.t. $|x_n - a_n| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.
For $n > N$, let $a_n = 0$ s.t. $|x_n| < \frac{\epsilon}{2}$.
Let $a = (a_n) \in A$.

$\Vert x_n - a_n \Vert_{\infty} = sup_n | x_n - a_n | \le max(max_{1\le n \le N}|x_n-a_n|, max_{n >N}|x_n|) = max(\frac{\epsilon}{2},\frac{\epsilon}{2}) = \frac{\epsilon}{2} < \epsilon$.
Thus, $a \in A$ approximates $x$ within $\epsilon \implies$$A$ is dense in $c_0(K)$. $\blacksquare$

Notes:
- $c_0(K)$ is the space of all null sequences i.e. of all sequences $x = (x_j)_{j=1}^{\infty} \in l^{\infty}(K)$ s.t. $x_j \to 0$ as $j \to \infty$.
- $c_0(K)$ is a subspace of $l^{\infty}(K)$ and for every $1 \le p < \infty$, the space $l^p(K)$ is a subspace of $c_0(K)$.
- A space to be separable is a _deeply important property_ in analysis and topology sicne it tells you that the space, despite being infinite-dimensional, is in a sense **not too large** — it can be **approximated** arbitrarily well using **countable data** (like rational sequences).
- If a space is separable, you can often replace infinite objects with **countable approximations**.
- In real-world applications (e.g. numerical analysis, machine learning), separability often justifies: using **finite-dimensional approximations** to infinite-dimensional problems; approximating functions or sequences using countable bases (e.g., wavelets, Fourier series).
