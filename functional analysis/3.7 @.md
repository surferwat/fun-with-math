Given any $1<p<\infty$, find a sequence $x$ such that $x \in l^p$ but $x \notin l^q$ for all $1 \le q<p$.

WTS: A sequence for which $(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|x_j|^p)^{\frac{1}{p}} < \infty$ and $(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|x_j|^q)^{\frac{1}{q}} = \infty$ for all  $1 \le q < p$.
In other words, we want to construct a decreasing sequence s.t. that it "just barely" lies in $l^p$ but diverges for all $q < p$.
A natural candidate is to use powers of $n$ with a logarithmic term to control convergence at the critical boundary.
Fix $p$ and let $x_j=\frac{1}{j^{1/p}(log j)^{\alpha}}$ for $j\ge2$ where $\alpha > 0$.
Then we have:
$(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|x_j|^p)^{\frac{1}{p}}= (\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1}{j^{1/p}(log j)^{\alpha}}|^p)^{\frac{1}{p}}=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1^p}{j^{p/p}(log j)^{\alpha p}}|)^{\frac{1}{p}}=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1}{j(log j)^{\alpha p}}|)^{\frac{1}{p}}$
$(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|x_j|^q)^{\frac{1}{q}}= (\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1}{j^{1/p}(log j)^{\alpha}}|^q)^{\frac{1}{q}}=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1^q}{j^{q/p}(log j)^{\alpha q}}|)^{\frac{1}{q}}=(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1}{j^{q/p}(log j)^{\alpha q}}|)^{\frac{1}{q}}$

We analyze $(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1}{j(log j)^{\alpha p}}|)^{\frac{1}{p}}$:
This series converges when $\alpha p > 1$ or $\alpha > 1/p$.
Thus, if $\alpha > 1/p$, then $x \in l^p$.

We analyze $(\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}|\frac{1}{j^{q/p}(log j)^{\alpha q}}|)^{\frac{1}{q}}$:
Since $q/p < 1$, the integral test tells us that this series diverges irregardless of $aq$.
In other words, $j^{q/p} \to \infty$ too slowly s.t. even with the log in the denominator, it's not enough to make this series converge.
Thus, $x \notin l^p$.

NOTES
This problem gives insight (1) the strictness of inclusions between $l^p$ spaces, (2) the existence of sequences that sit right on the boundary between different $l^p$ spaces, (3) the rate of decay required for membership in $l^p$, and (4) a deeper appreciation for the structure of $l^p$ spaces in functional analysis.
In proofs, it's sometimes essential to know when inclusions are strict so this type of sequence can be used as a counterexample to disprove implications
$\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{j}$ is known as the harmonic series or a special case of p-series with $p=1$ (where $p$ is not be confused with the $p$ used in the problem).
$\sum_{j=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{j(log j)^\beta}$ is known as a logarithmic series or a specifically, a "log-modified harmonic".
