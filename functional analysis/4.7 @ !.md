Show that if $f \in C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$ and $x \in C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$, then $\dot{x} = f(x)$, with $x(0) = x_0$, for all $t \in [0,T]$ if and only if $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$ for all $t \in [0,T]$.

$(\Rightarrow)$
Assume $\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))$ for all $t \in [0,T]$, $x(0)=x_0$, with $x \in C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$ and $f \in C(\mathbb{R}, \mathbb{R})$. 

Since $x$ is differentiable and $\dot{x}$ is continuous (as a composition of continuous functions), the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus applies. For any $t \in [0,T]$, $x(t) - x(0) = \int_{0}^{t} \dot{x}(s) \, ds$. 

Substituting $\dot{x}(s) = f(x(s))$ and $x(0) = x_0$, we obtain $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) \, ds$.

$(\Leftarrow)$
Now assume $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) \, ds$ for all $t \in [0,T]$, with $x \in C([0,T]; \mathbb{R}$).

Since $f$ and $x$ are continuous, the composition $f(x(\cdot))$ is continuous on $[0,T]$. By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, the function $t \mapsto \int_0^t f(x(s)) \, ds$ is differentiable, with derivative $f(x(t))$. Differentiating both sides yields $\dot{x}(t)=f(x(t))$ for all $t \in [0,T$]. Evaluating the integral expression at $t=0$ gives $x(0)=x_0$.

Conclusion
The function $x \in C([0,T]; \mathbb{R})$ satisfies $\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t))$, $x(0) = x_0$, for all $t \in [0,T]$ if and only if $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f((x(s)))  \, ds$ for all $t \in [0,T]$. This establishes the equivalence between the differential and integral formulations of the ODE.

Notes
- A solution of an ODE is not primarily about derivatives - it is about satisfying  a fixed-point integral relation.
- This problem proves solving $\dot{x}(t) = f(x(t))$, $x(0) = x_0$ is equivalent to solving $x(t) = x_0 + \int_0^t f(x(s)) \, ds$. These are called Differential (classical) Formulation and Integral (mild) Formulation. 
- Banach fixed-point theorem cannot see derivatives but it can see integrals.
- Integration produces differentiability, constants just shift the graph. This is why FTC is about the integral term, not the added constant so the emphasis on $t \mapsto \int_0^t f(x(s)) ds$ is precise (that's where FTC applies) and economical (the constant is irrelevant for derivatives).
