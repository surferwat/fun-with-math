Let $t=a-b$ be the Galois resolvent of an irreducible cubic treated in the preceding exercise. Under the assumption that the irreducible cubic has the special form $x^3+px+q$ (a change of variable puts any cubic in this form) find explicitly the $6$th degree polynomial $F(X)$ of which $t$ is a root. Show that $F(X)$ is reducible if the discriminant $-4p^3-27q^2$ is a square. (It will be seen in Exercise $5$ of the next set that $F(X)$ is reducible only if $-4p^3-27q^2$ is a square, in which case it is a product of two irreducible cubics.)

Let $f(x) = x^3 + px + q$ be an irreducible cubic over a field $K$ of characteristic $\ne 2, 3$ and let its roots be $r_1,r_2,r_3$. 
Then $r_1 + r_2 + r_3 = 0$, $r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3 = p$, $r_1r_2r_3 = -q$. 
Let $a = r_1 + \omega r_2 + \omega^2r_3$, $b= r_1 + \omega^2 r_2 + \omega r_3$, where $\omega$ is a primitive cube root of unity, and define the Galois resolvent $t=a-b$.
We determine the polynomial satisfied by $t$.

**Step 1: Compute $ab$**
Expand: $ab = (r_1 + \omega r_2 + \omega^2 r_3)(r_1 + \omega^2 r_2 + \omega r_3)$.
Using $1 + \omega + \omega^2 = 0$, we obtain $ab = r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2 - (r_1r_2+ r_1r_3 + r_2r_3)$.
Since $r_1+ r_2+ r_3 = 0$, $r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2 = (r_1 + r_2 + r_3)^2 - 2(r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3) = -2p$.
Hence $ab = -2p -p=-3p$.
Therefore, $ab = -3p$.

**Step 2: Compute $a^3 + b^3$**
A standard symmetric-function computation gives $a^3 + b^3 = -27q$.
So $a^3 + b^3 = -27q$.

**Step 3: Compute $t^2$**
Since $t=a-b$, we have $t^2 = (a-b)^2 = (a+b)^2 - 4ab$.
Now $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$, and $a^3 + b^3 = (a+b)(a^2 -ab + b^2)$.
Using $ab = -3p$, $a^3 + b^3 = -27q$, one derives the classical identity $t^2 = -3 \Delta$, where $\Delta = (r_1-r_2)^2(r_2-r_3)^2(r_3-r_1)^2$ is the discriminant of the cubic. 
For the depressed cubic $x^3 + px + q$, $\Delta = -4p^3 - 27q^2$. 
Therefore, $t^2 = -3(-4p^3 - 27q^2)$.
Thus, $t^2 = 12p^3 + 81q^2$.

**Step 4: Determine the polynomial $F(X)$**
The six conjugates of $t$ are $\pm t$, $\pm \omega t$, $\pm \omega^2 t$.
Hence the polynomial having these six roots is $F(X) = (X^2-t^2)(X^2 - \omega t^2)(X^2-\omega^2t^2)$.
Using $(Y-t^2)(Y-\omega t^2)(Y-\omega^2t^2) = Y^3 - t^6$, with $Y=X^2$, we get $F(X) = X^6 - t^6$. 
Now $t^2 = -3 \Delta$, so $t^6 = (-3)^3 \Delta^3 = -27 \Delta^3$.
Since $\Delta = -4p^3 -27q^2$, we conclude $F(X) = X^6 + 27(-4p^3-27q^2)^3$.
Equivalently, $F(X) = X^6 - 27(4p^3+27q^2)^3$.

**Step 5: Reducibility when the discriminant is a square**
Suppose $\Delta = -4p^3 - 27q^2$ is a square in $K$ say $\Delta = d^2$, $d \in \mathbb{K}$. 
Then $t^2 = -3d^2$, so $t^6 = (-27)d^6$.
Hence, $F(X) = X^6 - t^6$ so substituting the value of $t^6$, $F(X) = X^6+27d^6$.
Recognizing this as a sum of cubes, $F(X) = (X^2)^3 + (3d^2)^3$.
Using the identity $u^3 + v^3 = (u+v)(u^2-uv+v^2)$, with $u = X^2$ and $v = 3d^2$, we obtain $F(X) = (X^2 + 3d^2)(X^4 - 3d^2X^2+9d^4)$.
Sine $d \in \mathbb{K}$, this is a nontrivial factorization over $\mathbb{K}$, Hence $F(X)$ is reducible whenever the the discriminant $-4p^3-27q^2$ is a square in $\mathbb{K}$.

Notes to setup
- What does field $\mathbb{K}$ of characteristic $\ne 2, 3$ mean? A field is a number system where you can add, subtract, multiply, and divide (except by $0$). Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and finite fields like $F_5$. The characteristic of a field is the smallest positive integer $n$ s.t. $n \cdot 1 = 0$, if such an $n$ exists. For example:  $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, and finite fields like $F_5$, because $1+1+1+1+1 = 0$. So saying $char(\mathbb{K}) \ne 2,3$ means $2 \ne 0$ in the field, $3 \ne 0$ in the field. Why do we need this? Because the argument uses division by $2$ and $3$, and also uses cube roots of unity $\omega$ satisfying $\omega^2 + \omega + 1 = 0$. In characteristic $3$, this polynomial behaves badly: $x^2 + x + 1 = (x-1)^2$. So the whole resolvent construction degenerates in characteristics $2$ or $3$.
- Where do $p$ and $-q$ come from? The polynomial $f(x) = x^3 + px + q$. Suppose its roots are $r_1$, $r_2$, $r_3$. Then $f(x) = (x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3)$. Expanding: $(x-r_1)(x-r_2)(x-r_3) = x^3 - (r_1+r_2+r_3)x^2 + (r_1r_2 + r_1r_3+r_2r_3)x - r_1r_2r_3$. Now compare coefficients with $x^3 + px + q$. There is no $x^2$-term, so $r_1 + r_2 + r_3 = 0$. The coefficient of $x$ is $p$, so $r_1r_2 + r_1r_3 + r_2r_3 = p$. The constant term is $q$, so $-r_1r_2r_3 = -q$, hence $r_1r_2r_3 = -q$. These are exactly Viete's formuals. 
- Why define $a$ and $b$ this way? The expressions $a=r_1 + \omega r_2 + \omega^2 r_3$, $b=r_1 + \omega^2 r_2 + \omega r_3$ are chosen because they behave very nicely under permutations of the roots. The goal is encode the symmetry of the cubic, isolate information about the Galois group, build quantities whose cubes become symmetric. The key idea: diagonalizing permutations. Consider the cyclic permutation $(r_1, r_2, r_3) \mapsto (r_2, r_3, r_1)$. Apply it to $a$: $a \mapsto r_2 + \omega r_3 + \omega^2 r_1$. Factor out $\omega^2$: $= \omega^2(r_1 + \omega r_2 + \omega^2 r_3) = \omega^2 a$. So under the cycle (123), $a \mapsto \omega^2a$. Similarly, $b \mapsto \omega b$. This is extremely useful: instead of roots permuting messily, these combinations just get multiplied by scalars. This is analogous to diagonalizing a matrix in linear algebra. Why the cube? Since $(omega^2)^3=1$, we get $a^3 \mapsto a^3$. Thus, $a^3$ is invariant under cyclic permutations, making it much loser to something symmetric and expressible in terms of $p,q$. Indeed one finds: $a^3 + b^3 = -27q$, $a^3b^3 = (-3p)^3 = -27p^3$. So $a^3$, $b^3$ satisfy a quadratic equation determined entirely by $p, q$. This is the algebra behind Cardano's formula.
- Why define $t = a-b$? Because $a-b$ detects odd permutations. In fact, $t^2 = -3 \Delta$, where $\Delta = (r_1 - r_2)^2(r_2-r_3)^2(r_3-r_1)^2$ is the discriminant. So $t$ is essentially a square root of the discriminant. That is why the resolvent determines whether the Galois group is: $A_3$ (where $\Delta$ is a square), or $S_3$ (when it is not). So the construction of $a,b,t$ is not magic: it is designed to make permutations act as simply as possible. 

Notes to step 1 and step 2
- Once we define $t=a-b$, the natural strategy is to study $t$ through the symmetric functions of $a$ and $b$. The point is that $a$ and $b$ themselves are not fixed by the Galois group, but the symmetric combinations $a+b$, $ab$, $a^3+b^3$, ... often are. Those can therefore be expressed in terms of the coefficients $p$, $q$. Directly expanding powers of $a-b$ is messy, but $a$ and $b$ come as a pair, and symmetric expressions in a pair are controllable. Then the standard identity immediately suggests itself: $(a-b)^2 = (a+b)^2-4ab$ and $a^3+b^3 = (a+b)^3 -3ab(a+b)$. So , if we can compute  symmetric quantities $a^3+b^3$ and $ab$, then we can determine $(a+b)^2$ and hence, $t^2=(a-b)^2$.
- In this problem: $ab=-3p$ and $a^3+b^3 = -27q$ both are expressible in terms of the coefficients of the cubic.

Notes
- The resolvent detects the Galois group. For an irreducible cubic over $\mathbb{K}$, the Galois group is either $S_3$ or $A_3$. The resolvent $t = a-b$ is designed so that: odd permutations send $t \mapsto -t$, cyclic permutations multiply $t$ by $\omega$. So the behavior of $t$ encodes the permutation structure of the roots. The six conjugates $\pm t$, $\pm \omega t$, $\pm \omega^2 t$ mirror the six elements of $S_3$.
- The discriminant controls reducibility. The key identity is $t^2 = -3 \Delta$. Thus if $\Delta$ is a square in $\mathbb{K}$, then $t^2 \in \mathbb{K}$ is already a square up to the constant $-3$, the Galois group drops from $S_3$ to $A_3$, the degree-$6$ resolvent polynomial becomes reducible. This is one of the earliest examples of a general principle: "The discriminant measures whether odd permutations survive in the Galois group". For cubics: $\Delta$ square $\iff Gal(f) \subset A_3$. Since the cubic is irreducible, this means $Gal(f) \cong A_3 \cong C_3$. Otherwise the Galois group is $S_3$.
