Prove Galois' Proposition III (also added to his treatise at a later time, possibly on the night before his duel): If, in Proposition II, all roots of the auxiliary equations are adjoined to $K$ then the new group is a normal subgroup of the old.

To adjoin all the roots of the auxiliary equation is the same as adjoining a Galois resolvent $u$ of the auxiliary equation $g(x)=0$ over $K$.
Let $u,u',u'',\ldots$ be all the conjugates of $u$ over $K$, and let their number be $\mu$.

Since $u$ is a Galois resolvent, every conjugate $u'$ is a rational function of $u$. 
Thus all the fields $K(u),K(u'),K(u''),\ldots$ are the same field. 
Consequently, the polynomials $H(X,u),H(X,u'),H(X,u''),\ldots$ all have their coefficients in the same field $K(u)$, and they are irreducible over that field.
Therefore, if two of these polynomials have a root in common, they must have the same roots: two irreducible polynomials over a field which have a common root have the same irreducible factor, and hence, since they have the same degree, they are identical up to a constant factor.
As in §44, multiplying all these polynomials gives $\prod H(X,u^{(i)})=\operatorname{const.},G(X)^j$ for some integer $j$. 
Since the $H$'s have simple roots, every root of $G(X)$ occurs in exactly $j$ of the $H$'s.
We may therefore rearrange the $u$'s so that the factors can be grouped according to their common roots. 
Thus $G(X)=\operatorname{const.},H(X,u)H(X,u')H(X,u'')\cdots H(X,u^{(\mu/j)}).$

Here $\mu$ is the number of conjugates of $u$. 
This equation says that the roots of $G(X)$, and hence the presentations of the old group, are partitioned into $\mu/j$ sets, each set giving a presentation of the new group.
Now the conjugates of $u$ are permuted by every substitution of the old group. 
Hence an old-group substitution permutes these $\mu/j$ presentations of the new group.
In particular, conjugating the new group by any element of the old group gives one of the presentations occurring in this partition.
But all these presentations represent the same subgroup: they are obtained from the same new group by conjugation with elements of the old group. 
Thus the new group is carried into itself by every substitution of the old group.
Therefore the new group is a normal subgroup of the old group.

Author's solution:
To adjoin all roots is the same as to adjoin a Galois resolvent $u \text{ of } g(x)=0 \text{ over } K$. Any other root $u'$ of the irreducible polynomial satisfied by $u$ is a rational function of $u$. Therefore the polynomials $H(X,u), H(X, u'), H(X,u''), \dots$ all have coefficients in the same field $K(u)$ and are irreducible over that field. Therefore, any two of them which have a root in common have identical roots. As in $\S44$, their product is const. $G(X)^j$ for some $j$. Since the $H$'s have simple roots , every root of $G$ is a root of exactly $j$ of the $H$'s. Therefore the $u$'s can be rearranged, if necessary, so that $G(X) = \text{ const. } H(X,u)H(X,u')H(X,u'') \dots H(X,u^{(\mu/j)})$, where $\mu$ is the number of $u$'s. This partitions the presentation of the old group into $\mu/j$ presentations of the new, and the proof that the new is a normal subgroup of the old is as in $\S44$.

Notes:
- Since each conjugate $u'$ is a rational function of $u$, we have $u' \in K(u)$, and since $u$ is likewise a rational function of $u'$, we have $u \in K(u')$; hence $K(u) = K(u')$, and similarly for all conjugates.
- Let $d = \deg H$, $n = \deg G$. The polynomial $H(X,u)$ has $d$ roots. Each of those roots occurs in exactly $j$ of the $H$'s. Therefore, the group of $H$'s containing those roots consists of $j$ copies for each root - but because each $H$ contains all the same roots once, this gives a group whose total product contains each of those roots with multiplicity $j$. More directly, from $\mu \deg H = j \deg G$, we get $\mu / j = \deg G / \deg H$. Thus exactly $\mu/j$ of the $H's$ are needed to account for all the roots of $G$ once. So after rearranging the $H$'s, we can take the first $\mu/j$ of them so that together they contain every root of $G$ exactly once. Hence their product must be $G(X)$, up to a constant: $G(X) = \text{ const }. H(X,u)H(X,u')\dots H(X,u^(\mu/j))$. Think of it this way: $\underbrace{H_1H_2 \dots H_{\mu/j}}_{\text{all roots of }G \text{ once}}$  $\underbrace{H_{\mu/j+1} \dots H_{2\mu/j}}_{\text{all roots of }G \text{ once}} \dots$. There are $j$ such groups, because $j\binom{\mu}{j} = \mu$.  And therefore the whole product is $G(X)^j$ up to a constant. So "each group of $H$'s corresponds to one copy of $G(X)$" means that each group contains every root of $G$ exactly once, and therefore its product is $G(X)$ up to a constant.
