Show that if one root of an irreducible equation can be expressed in terms of radicals then all roots can. Let $K,K'$ be as in the proposition of $\S44$ and let $f$ be a polynomial with coefficients in $K$ which is irreducible over $K$. Show that if $f = g_1, g_2, ... g_k$ is a factorization of $f$ in factors irreducible over $K'$ and if all roots of $g_1$ can be expressed in terms of radicals then all roots of $f$ can be expressed in terms of radicals.

Let $f(x)\in K[x]$ be irreducible over $K$. If one root of $f$ can be expressed in terms of radicals, then every root of $f$ can be expressed in terms of radicals.

It is enough to prove the following lemma.

> **Lemma.** Let $K'=K(r)$, where $r^p\in K$ and $K$ contains the $p$th roots of unity. Suppose $f=g_1g_2\cdots g_k$ is the factorization of the irreducible polynomial $f$ into factors irreducible over $K'$. If all roots of $g_1$ can be expressed in terms of radicals, then all roots of $f$ can be expressed in terms of radicals.

$\mathit{proof \, of \, lemma}$
If $g_1=f$, there is nothing to prove. 

Assume therefore that $f$ factors over $K'$.
By the argument of §44, there is a polynomial $g(x,y)\in K[x,y]$ such that $g_1(x)=g(x,r)$, and
$f(x)=g(x,r)g(x,\alpha r)g(x,\alpha^2r)\cdots g(x,\alpha^{p-1}r),$ where $\alpha$ is a primitive $p$th root of unity. 
Indeed, the lemma of §44 implies that whenever $g(x,r)$ divides $f(x)$, every conjugate polynomial $g(x,\alpha^ir)$ also divides $f(x)$.

Since $f$ is irreducible over $K$, there is a substitution $T$ in the Galois group of $f$ over $K$ satisfying $T(r)=\alpha r$. 
Hence $T^j(g(x,r))=g(x,\alpha^jr)$, so $T^j$ carries the roots of $g(x,r)$ onto the roots of $g(x,\alpha^jr)$.

Now let $u$ be a Galois resolvent for $g(x,r)$ over $K'$. 
Applying $T^j$ to the entire construction gives the resolvent $T^ju$ for $g(x,\alpha^jr)$. 
Thus the Galois group of $g(x,\alpha^jr)$ is obtained from that of $g(x,r)$ simply by conjugating the construction by $T^j$. 
In particular, the two Galois groups are isomorphic and therefore have the same solvability properties.

Since all roots of $g_1=g(x,r)$ are expressible in terms of radicals, its Galois group over $K'$ is solvable. 
Consequently, every conjugate factor $g(x,\alpha^jr)$ also has solvable Galois group, so all of its roots are expressible in terms of radicals.

The roots of $f$ are precisely the union of the roots of the factors $g(x,r),g(x,\alpha r),\ldots,g(x,\alpha^{p-1}r)$, and therefore every root of $f$ is expressible in terms of radicals.  $\square$

$\mathit{proof \, of \, theorem}$
Returning to the theorem, suppose one root $\beta$ of the irreducible polynomial $f$ is expressible in terms of radicals. 
Then there is a tower of radical extensions
$K=K_0\subset K_1\subset\cdots\subset K_n,$ where each extension is of the type considered in Proposition §44, and $\beta\in K_n$.

Over $K_n$, the polynomial $f$ has the linear factor $x-\beta$, whose unique root is expressible in terms of radicals. 
Applying the lemma to the extension $K_{n-1}\subset K_n$ shows that every root of every irreducible factor over $K_{n-1}$ is expressible in terms of radicals. 
Repeating the argument successively for $K_{n-2}\subset K_{n-1},\ldots,K_0\subset K_1$, one descends the radical tower step by step. 
At the bottom of the tower, $f$ is irreducible over $K$, so every root of $f$ is expressible in terms of radicals.

Therefore, if one root of an irreducible polynomial can be expressed in terms of radicals, then all of its roots can be expressed in terms of radicals. $\square$

Author's solution:
The suggested lemma proves the theorem, because a succession of extensions like the one of the proposition of $\S44$ gives, by assumption, a root of the given irreducible polynomial and hence an irreducible (linear) factor whose roots can be expressed in terms of radicals. Thus all roots of one of the irreducible factors at the next-to-last stage can be expressed in terms of radicals, and so forth, until one arrives at the desired conclusion. The lemma says, in effect, that if all roots $g_1$ can be expressed in terms of radicals then so can all roots of $g_i$ for all $i$. In other words, if the Galois group of $g_1$ over $K'$ is solvable then so is the group of $g_i$ over $K'$. If $g_i \ne f$ then adaptations of arguments of $\S44$ show that $f(x) = g(x,r)(g(x, \alpha r)g(x, \alpha^2 r)...g(x, \alpha^{p-1} r)$ where $g(x,y)$ is a polynomial in two variables with coefficients in $K$ because $f$ is irreducible over $K'=K(r)$. Moreover, $g(x, \alpha r) = g(x, Tr)$ for some substitution $T$ of the Galois group of $f(x)=0$ over $K$ because $f$ is irreducible over $K$. Thus, given any two of the polynomials $g(x, \alpha^j r)$ there is a suitable power $T^j \text{ of } T$ which carries roots of one to roots of the other and leaves elements of $K$ fixed. If $u$ is a Galois resolvent of the first (over $K'$) then $T^ju$ is a Galois resolvent of the first, $T^j$ applied to this construction gives a presentation of the Galois group of the second. Therefore a sequence of subgroups of the required type for the first Galois group implies one for the second.

$\S44$ proposition and lemma
Proposition. Consider, as above, the Galois group of an equation $f(x)=0$ over the field $K$. Let $p$ be a prime, let $K$ contain $p$th roots of unity, and let $K' \supset K$ be the extension of $K$ obtained by adjoining the $p$th root of an element $k \text{ of } K$. Then, the new Galois group (that of $f(x) = 0$ over $K'$) is either the same as the old one or it is a normal subgroup of index $p$.
