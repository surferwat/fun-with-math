Show that if $a,b,c$ are the roots of an irreducible cubic equation with rational coefficients then $a$-$b$ is a Galois resolvent of the equation. [If an equation has a rational  root it is not irreducible, nor is it irreducible - by Exercise 2 - if it has multiple roots.]

Let $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ be an irreducible cubic with roots $a,b,c$ in some splitting field $K$.

**Step 1: Basic consequences of irreducibility**
Since $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$:
- $f$ has no rational roots.
- $f$ has no repeated roots, so the roots $a,b,c$ are distinct.
Let $G = Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})$ be the Galois group of the splitting field.
Because $f$ is irreducible, the roots are all conjugate over $\mathbb{Q}$. Therefore the Galois group acts transitively on the set $\{a,b,c\}$.
Thus $G$ is a transitive subgroup of $S_3$, so: $G \cong A_3$ or $S_3$.

**Step 2: Consider the element $a-b$**
We study how elements of $G$ act on the quantity $a-b$.
For any $\sigma \in G$, $\sigma(a-b) = \sigma(a) - \sigma(b)$.
Since $\sigma$ permutes the roots $a,b,c$, the possible images of $a-b$ are among: $a-b$, $b-a$, $a-c$, $c-a$, $b-c$, $c-b$.
Thus the orbit of $a-b$ under $G$ is contained in this set.

**Step 3: These differences are distinct**
We claim that the six quantaties $\pm(a-b), \pm(a-c) \pm (b-c)$ are distinct.
Certainly, $a-b=b-a$, would imply $a=b$, contradicting the fact that the roots are distinct.
Now consider a "mixed" equality such as $a-b = c-a$.
Then $2a = b+ c$.
Since $a+b+c \in \mathbb{Q}$ (by Vieta's formulas), we obtain $3a = a+b+c \in \mathbb{Q}$.
Hence $a \in \mathbb{Q}$, contradicting irreducibility of the cubic.
The same argument applies to any other possible equality among these differences. Therefore all six differences are distinct.

**Step 4: Conjugates of $a-b$**
The conjugates of $a-b$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ are precisely the elements in its orbit under $G$:
- If $G \cong S_3$, all six differences occur: $\{\pm(a-b), \pm(a-c), \pm(b-c)\}$.
- If $G \cong A_3$, only the three cyclic differences occur: $a-b$, $b-c$, $c-a$.
In either case, every conjugate of $a-b$ is obtained by permuting the roots.
Therefore the minimal polynomial of $a-b$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ has degree: $6$ if $G \cong S_3$, and $3$ if $G \cong A_3$.

**Step 5: Resolvent property**
A Galois resolvent is an expression in the roots whose conjugates are obtained by the action of the Galois group permuting the roots.
The element $a-b$ has exactly this property:
- it is transformed by permutations of the roots,
- Its conjugates are precisely the values obtained from these permutations,
- and these conjugates reflect the structure of the Galois group.
Thus $a-b$ is a Galois resolvent of the cubic polynomial.

**Conclusion**
Since the conjugates of $a-b$ are exactly the distinct expressions obtained by permuting the roots, the element $a-b$ satisfies the defining property of a Galois resolvent. 
Therefore, $a-b$ is a Galois resolvent of the irreducible cubic.

Notes
- This problem is significant because it illustrates the central idea of Galois theory: study a polynomial buy studying how symmetries permute its roots. The quantity $a-b$ is not chosen randomly. It captures information about how the Galois group acts on the roots, and from its behavior you can recover the structure of the group. Historically, resolvents were used to solve polynomial equations before modern abstract algebra existed. For cubics and quartics, mathematicians found special combinations of roots whose symmetries were easier to analyze. These auxiliary quantities reduced complicated permutation behavior to something manageable. This eventually led Galois to the insight: solvability of equations depends on the structure of the permutation group of the roots.
- A polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ if it cannot be factor into lower-degree polynomials with rational coefficients.So, $f(x)$ irreducible $\iff$ $f(x) \ne g(x)h(x)$ for any non-constant $g,h \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$.
- $Gal(K/\mathbb{Q})$ is a set of functions specifically a set of field automorphisms of $K$ that fix every element of $\mathbb{Q}$. 
- The roots are all conjugate over $\mathbb{Q}$ means that if $a,b,c$ are roots of the same irreducible polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$, then there exist field automorphisms of the splitting field $K$ fixing $\mathbb{Q}$ that send any root to any other root.
- If $a \in \mathbb{Q}$, then $a$ is a rational root of the polynomial $f(x)$. But a polynomial over $\mathbb{Q}$ with a rational root is reducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Indeed by the factor theorem, $f(a) = 0 \Rightarrow f(x) = (x-a)g(x)$ for some $g(x) \in \mathbb{Q}[x]$.
