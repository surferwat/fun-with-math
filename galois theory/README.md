## Overview
Galois Theory by Harold Edwards is an introduction to Galois theory along the lines of Galois' "Memoir on the Conditions for Solvability of Equations by Radicals". Galois theory is a branch of abstract algebra that connects field theory with group theory. At its heart, Galois theory connects two seemingly different ways of studying polynomial equations: the fields generated by their roots and the symmetries that permute those roots. Galois used this connection to characterize which polynomial equations can be solved by radicals in terms of the structure of the permutation group of their roots. An equation is, by definition, solvable by radicals if its roots can be expressed using integers, nth roots, and the four basic arithmetic operations.

The textbook can essentially be split into two parts: the first part $\S1 - \S48$ and the second part $\S49 - \S71$. The first part covers some of the ideas that preceded Galois theory as well as the basic theory itself, including Galois groups and the groups associated with solvable equations. The second part develops a firmer foundation for the theory and explores some of its applications.

What follows is a selection of key statements and exercises from the first part of the textbook. For each statement, I first give the formal mathematical formulation and then rewrite the basic idea and include an example to illustrate how the abstract concepts work in concrete cases.

## Key Definitions and Statements
### The Galois Group
#### Galois Resolvent
Let $K$ be the field of quantities that are assumed to be known, and let $f(x)=0$ be a polynomial equation of degree $n$ with coefficients in $K$. Let $a,b,c, \dots$ be the roots of $f(x)=0$. It will be assumed that the roots $a,b,c, \dots$ are distinct, a restriction which does not cost any generality because there is a simple technique for replacing an equation $f(x)=0$ with multiple roots with another equation with the same roots, all occurring once. Let $K(a,b,c, \dots)$ denote the field of all elements that can be expressed as rational functions of the roots $a,b,c, \dots$. Then there is an element $t \in K(a,b,c, \dots)$, called a Galois resolvent, with the property that any given element of $K(a,b,c, \dots)$ can be expressed as a polynomial in $t$ with coefficients in $K$. In fact, $t$ can be taken to have the form $t=Aa +Bb+Cc+ \dots$ where $A,B,C, \dots$ are integers. Let $F(X)$ be the polynomial $F(X)= \prod (X-ASa-BSb-CSc- \dots)$ with coefficients in $K$, where the product is over all $n!$ permutations $S$ of the roots $a,b,c, \dots$, and let $F(X)=G_1(X)G_2(X) \dots G_k(X)$ be a decomposition of $F$ into irreducible factors. Then $F(t)=0$, and, consequently, $t$ is a root of one of the polynomials $G_i$, say $G_t(t)=0$. Then the field $K(t)$, which is isomorphic to $K(a,b,c, \dots)$ can be represented very concretely as the simple algebraic extension of $K$ obtained by adjoining a root of the irreducible polynomial $G_1$.

>**Basic idea**
>A Galois resolvent is a specially chosen combination of the roots that generates the same field as all the roots. Thus, instead of working with the whole collection of roots, we can work with the single number t without losing any of the algebraic information contained in the field they generate.
> 
>**Example**
>Suppose $f(x)=0$ is a cubic polynomial with roots $a,b,c$. 
>We want to work with the field $K(a,b,c)$, but dealing with three roots can be complicated.
>We choose a special combination of the roots, for example $t=Aa+Bb+Cc$, where the integers $A,B,C$ are chosen appropriately. For a suitable choice, $t$ is a Galois resolvent, meaning that $K(t)=K(a,b,c)$. For example, we might take $t=a+2b+3c$.
>If $t$ is chosen appropriately, it is a Galois resolvent, meaning $K(t)=K(a,b,c)$. 
>So instead of working with three roots $a,b,c,$ we can work with just one number $t$.
>To find a polynomial that $t$ satisfies, consider all six permutations of $a,b,c$ in $a+2b+3c$:
>$t_1 = a+2b+3c,$
>$t_2 = a+2c+3b,$
>$\dots,$
>$t_6 = c+2b+3a.$
>Construct $F(X)=\prod_{i=1}^6(X-t_i)$ which is its factorization over a field containing all the $t_i's$ (in this case $K(a,b,c)$.
>Our chosen $t$ is one of the $t_i's$ so $F(t)=0$.
>Since $F(X)$ has coefficients in $K$, we can factor $F(X)$ into irreducible polynomials over $K$: $F(X)=G_1(X)G_2(X)...G_k(X)$.
>Our $t$ is a root of one of these factors, say $G_1(t)=0$.
>Then $K(t)=K(a,b,c)$. In other words, instead of working with all the roots $a,b,c$, we can work with the single number $t$. The entire field can be represented concretely as the simple algebraic extension of $K$ obtained by adjoining $t$, a root of the irreducible polynomial $G_1​(X)$.

#### Galois Group of the Field
The Galois group of the given equation $f(x)=0$ with roots $a,b,c,...$ is the group with the following presentation. Let each of the roots $a,b,c,...$ be expressed in terms of the Galois resolvent $t$, say $a=\phi_a(t), b=\phi_b(t), c = \phi_c(t), ...,$ where the $\phi$'s are polynomials with coefficients in $K$. Let $F(X)$ be the polynomial of degree $n!$ whose roots are the $n!$ distinct elements $ASa+BSb+CSc+ ...$ of $K(a,b,c,...)$ where $t=Aa+Bb+Cc + ...$ and where $S$ is one of the $n!$ substitutions of $a,b,c,...$ Finally, let $G(X)$ be an irreducible (over $K$) factor of $F(X)$ of which $t$ is a root. The conjugates $t',t'', \dots$ of $t$ (over $K$) are the other roots of $G(X)$. The Galois group of $f(x)=0$ is presented by 

$\phi_a(t), \phi_b(t), \phi_c(t),...$,
$\phi_a(t'), \phi_b(t'), \phi_c(t'),...$,
$\phi_a(t''), \phi_b(t''), \phi_c(t''),...$,
$\dots$

The number of rows in the table - and therefore the number of elements in the group.- is equal to the number of roots $t,t',t'', \dots$ of $G$, which is equal to $\mathrm{deg} G$. (Note that the conjugates $t',t'',\dots)$ are all roots of $F$ and are therefore of the form $ASa+BSb+CSc+\dots$ where $S$ is a substitution of $a,b,c, \dots)$

>**Basic idea** 
>The Galois group is the collection of all the ways we can permute the roots of a polynomial while preserving all the algebraic relationships between them and keeping the elements of the base field $K$ fixed (i.e., every element of $K$ remains unchanged under the permutation). If $\sigma$ is an element of the Galois group, then $\sigma(k)=k$ for every $k \in K$, while $\sigma$ may send one root to another root, provided it preserves addition and multiplication.
>
>**Example**
>Let $x^3-2=0$.
>Suppose $f(x) = x^3 -2$ has roots $a=\sqrt[3]{2}$,  $b=a \omega$,  $c=a \omega^2$, where $\omega^3=1$.
>The Galois group consists of the algebraically valid ways of rearranging these three roots while keeping every rational number fixed.
>For example, one possible rearrangement is $a \to b$, $b \to c$, $c \to a$.
>Another is $a \to a$, $b \to c$, $c \to b$.
>There are six such valid rearrangements in total. 
>Therefore, the Galois group of $x^3−2$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ consists of the six algebraically valid rearrangements of its three roots.

#### Proposition I
Let $\Psi(U, V, W, ...)$ be a polynomial in $n$ variables with coefficients in $K$. Let $\Psi_{t'}$ be the element of $K(a,b,c,...)$ obtained by setting $U = \phi_a(t'), V = \phi_b(t'), W = \phi_c(t'), ...$ in $\Psi$. In other words, for $U,V,W,...$ one substitutes the arrangement of the roots given in the row of the above table $(1)$ corresponding to $t'$. Similarly, let $\Psi_t, \Psi_{t''}, \Psi_{t'''}, ...$ be defined by substituting the corresponding row. Then $\Psi_t$ is in $K$ if and only if the elements $\Psi_t, \Psi_{t'}, \Psi_{t''}, ...$ are all equal. Loosely speaking, $\Psi(a,b,c,...)$ is a known quantity if and only if it is invariant under all the substitutions of the Galois group.

>**Basic idea** 
>If every allowed rearrangement of the roots gives the same value, that value is in the base field $K$. If the rearrangements change the value, it is not in $K$.
>
>**Example**
>Take $f(x)=x^3-2$ over $K=\mathbb{Q}$, with roots $a=\sqrt[3]{2}, b=a\omega, c=a\omega^2$.
>Suppose our Galois resolvent is chosen so that the different conjugates $t,t',t'',\ldots$ correspond to the different permutations of $a,b,c$.
>
>Let's choose $\psi(U,V,W)=U+V+W$.
>We want to know whether $\psi(a,b,c)=a+b+c$ belongs to $\mathbb{Q}$.
>One row of the Galois table is $(a,b,c)$, so $\psi_t=a+b+c$.
>Another row might be $(b,c,a)$, so $\psi_{t'}=b+c+a$.
>Another row might be $(c,a,b)$, so $\psi_{t''}=c+a+b$.
>But addition is commutative, so $a+b+c=b+c+a=c+a+b$.
>In fact, every one of the six permutations gives the same answer:
$\psi_t=\psi_{t'}=\psi_{t''}=\cdots=a+b+c$.
>Therefore, by Proposition I, $a+b+c\in\mathbb{Q}$.
>Indeed, by Vieta's formula, $a+b+c=0$.
>So $\psi(a,b,c)=0\in\mathbb{Q}$.
>
>Now compare with $\psi(U,V,W)=U$.
>We want to know whether $\psi(a,b,c)=a$ belongs to $\mathbb{Q}$.
>For the same Galois table, $\psi_t=a,\quad \psi_{t'}=b,\quad \psi_{t''}=c$.
>These are not all equal. 
>Therefore, $\psi_t$ is not invariant under the Galois group.
>By Proposition I, an element belongs to $K=\mathbb{Q}$ if and only if it is unchanged by all the Galois-group substitutions. 
>Hence $\psi_t\notin\mathbb{Q}$.
>Since $\psi_t=a$,$a\notin\mathbb{Q}$.

### The Groups of Solvable Equations
#### Proposition II
Consider, as above, the Galois group of an equation $f(x)=0$ over the field $K$. Let $p$ be a prime, let $K$ contain $p$th roots of unity, and let $K' \supset K$ be the extension of $K$ obtained by adjoining the $p$th root of an element $k$ of $K$. Then the new Galois group (that of $f(x)=0$ over $K'$) is either the same as the old one or it is a normal subgroup of index $p$.

>**Basic idea** 
>Suppose we start with a polynomial $f(x)=0$ over $K$, and then enlarge the field by adding a $p$th root of some element $k \in K$. If $K$ already contains all the $p$th roots of unity, then adding this one new $p$th root can affect the Galois group in only two ways: **either the Galois group does not change at all, or the new Galois group becomes a normal subgroup of the old Galois group whose index is $p$**. Thus, adjoining a $p$th root can reduce the number of allowed permutations of the roots, but it can reduce them only by a factor of $p$. Enlarging the field by adding a $p$th root of some element $k \in K$ is a special kind of field extension for which Proposition II gives us a specific conclusion: $\mathrm{Gal}(f/K') = \mathrm{Gal}(f/K)$ or $\mathrm{Gal}(f/K') \triangleleft \mathrm{Gal}(f/K)$ with index $p$.
>
>**Example**
>Let $K=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, where is $\omega$ is a primitive cube root of unity, so that $\omega^3=1, \omega \ne 1$. 
>Consider $f(x)=x^3-2$.
>Its roots are $a=\sqrt[3]{2}, b = a \omega, c = a \omega^2$. 
>Over $K = \mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, the Galois group of $f(x)$ has three elements: 
>$a \mapsto a, b \mapsto b, c \mapsto c,$
>$a \mapsto b, b \mapsto c, c \mapsto a,$
>$a \mapsto c, b \mapsto a, c \mapsto b.$
>To illustrate Proposition II, return to the polynomial $f(x)=x^3-2$.
>Since we are adjoining a cube root, we take $p=3$.
>We then enlarge $K$ by adjoining the cube root of $2$: $K' = K(\sqrt[3]{2}) = \mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2})$.
>Since $K$ already contains the third roots of unity $1, \omega, \omega^2$, Proposition II applies.
>But once $\sqrt[3]{2} = a$ has been added to the base field, $a$ must remain fixed by every substitution over $K'$. 
>Since $b=a \omega, c= a \omega^2$, and both $a$ and $\omega$ are now in $K'$, the other roots $b$ and $c$ are also already in $K'$. 
>Therefore every root of $f$ is fixed, and the new Galois group is just ${1}$.
>The old group had $3$ elements, while the new group has $1$ element.
>Hence $[Gal(f/K):Gal(f/K')]=3=p$.
>Morever, the trivial group is a normal subgroup of the old group.
>Thus, in this example, adjoining the cube roots reduces the Galois group to a normal subgroup of index $3$, exactly as Proposition II predicts.

#### Lemma
If $U(X,r)=V(X,r)W(X,r)$ where $U,V,$ and $W$ are polynomials in two variables with coefficients in $K ($so that $U(X,r),V(X,r)$, and $W(X,r)$  are polynomials in one variable $X$ with coefficients in $K(r)=K')$ then $U(X, \alpha^i r)=V(X, \alpha^i r) W(X, \alpha^i r)$ for all $i=1,2, \dots$.

>**Basic idea** 
>If $U(X,r)=V(X,r)W(X,r)$, then the same factorization holds when $r$ is replaced by any of its conjugates $α^ir$. Since these conjugates are exactly the values obtained by the Galois substitutions of $r$, the factorization $U=VW$ is preserved under every Galois substitution.
>
>**Example**
>Let's temporarily take $K=\mathbb{Q}$, and let $r=\sqrt{2}$.
>Its conjugate over $\mathbb{Q}$ is $-r$.
>So here the role of $\alpha$ is played by $-1$.
>Consider the polynomial $U(X,r)=X^2-r^2$.
>We can factor it as $U(X,r)=(X-r)(X+r)$.
>Thus let $V(X,r)=X-r$ and $W(X,r)=X+r$.
>We have the identity $U(X,r)=V(X,r)W(X,r)$.
>Now replace $r$ by its conjugate $-r$.
>The lemma tells us that $U(X,-r)=V(X,-r)W(X,-r)$.
>Let's check: $U(X,-r)=X^2-(-r)^2=X^2-r^2$, while $V(X,-r)=X-(-r)=X+r$ and $W(X,-r)=X+(-r)=X-r$.
>Therefore $U(X,-r)=(X+r)(X-r)$.
>And of course, $(X+r)(X-r)=X^2-r^2$.
>So the factorization has simply been conjugated: $(X-r)(X+r) \mapsto (X+r)(X-r)$.

#### Proposition III
Let $G$ be the Galois group of $f(x)=0$ over $K$ and let $G'$ be a normal subgroup of $G$ of prime index $p$. Assume also that $K$ contains a primitive $p$th root of unity $\alpha$, that is, a solution of $\alpha^p = 1$ with $\alpha \ne 1$. Then there is an element $k$ of $K$ such that if $K' \supset K$ is the field obtained by adjoining a $p$th root of $k$ to $K$ then the Galois group of $f(x)=0$ over $K'$ is $G'$.

>**Basic idea** 
>If you can identify a normal subgroup $G'$ of the Galois group $G$ whose index is a prime number $p$, then you can shrink the Galois group from $G$ to exactly $G'$ by adjoining one suitable $p$th root to the base field. If there is a normal subgroup that is smaller by a factor of $p$, then we can find a $p$th root that causes exactly that reduction. This is an important idea behind solvability by radicals: we can progressively enlarge the field by adjoining roots, and each enlargement can correspond to removing a layer of the Galois group.
>
>**Example**
>Take $f(x)=x^3-2$ over $K=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ where $\omega$ is a primitive cube root of unity: $\omega^3=1$, $\omega \ne 1$.
>The roots are $a=\sqrt[3]{2}, b=\omega \sqrt[3]{2}, c= \omega^2\sqrt[3]{2}$.
>Over $K=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, the Galois group has three elements: $G=\lbrace1, \sigma, \sigma^2 \rbrace$, where, for example, $\sigma(a)=b \sigma(b)=c, \sigma(c)=a$
>So $\vert G \vert = 3$.
>Now consider the subgroup $G'=\left\{1 \right\}$.
>This is a normal subgroup of $G$, and $[G:G'] = \frac{3}{1}=3$.
>Thus $G'$ has prime index $p=3$ in $G$.
>Also, our field $K=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$ already contains a primitive cube root of unity, so all the conditions of the proposition are satisfied.
>The proposition now tells us: There must be some $k\ \in K$ such that adjoining $\sqrt[3]{k}$ to $K$ changes the Galois group from $G$ to $G'$.
>In this example, we can choose $k=2$.
>Then $K'=K(\sqrt[3]{2})=\mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2})$.
>But once we have added $a=\sqrt[3]{2}$, we automatically have $b=a \omega$ and $c=a \omega^2$, because both $a$ and $\omega$ are now in $K'$.
>Therefore all three roots are already in $K'$.
>Consequently, every automorphism fixing $K'$ must fix $a,b,c$.
>There are no nontrivial permutations left.
>Hence $\mathrm{Gal}(f/K')=\left\{1\right\}=G'$.

#### Theorem
Let $f(x)=0$ be an equation with distinct roots whose Galois group $G$ over the field $K$ is solvable, that is, has a sequence of subgroups $G \supset G_1 \supset G_2 \supset \dots \supset G_v$ in which each $G_i$ is a normal subgroup of prime index in its predecessor and $G_v$ contains the identity substitutions alone. Then $f(x)=0$ can be solved by radicals, that is, one can construct a sequence of field extensions $K \subset K' \subset K'' \subset \dots \subset K^{(\mu)}$ such that $f(x)=0$ has $n=\mathrm{deg} f$ roots in $K^{(\mu)}$ and such that the elements of any $K^{(i)}$ can be expressed rationally in terms of elements of its predecessor and a fixed radical of a fixed element of its predecessor - in fact the elements can be expressed as polynomials in the radical with coefficients in $K^{(i-1)}$.


>**Basic idea** 
>A solvable Galois group means that the symmetries of the roots can be removed one prime-sized layer at a time. Each layer can be removed by adjoining an appropriate radical. Once all the symmetries are gone, all the roots are present. Therefore the polynomial can be solved using radicals.
>
>**Example**
>Consider $f(x)=x^3-2$ over $K=\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, where $\omega$ is a primitive cube root of unity: $\omega^3=1$, $\omega \ne 1$.
>The three roots are $a=\sqrt[3]{2}, b=\omega \sqrt[3]{2}, c=\omega^2 \sqrt[3]{2}$.
>Step 1: Identify the Galois group
>Over $\mathbb{Q}(\omega)$, the Galois group consists of the three cyclic permutations $1, \sigma, \sigma^2$, where $\sigma(a)=b, \sigma(b)=c, \sigma(c)=a$.
>Thus $\vert G \vert = 3$. 
>Since $3$ is prime, we have the subgroup chain $G \supset \left\{1\right\}$.
>The subgroup $\left\{1\right\}$ is normal in $G$, and $[G:\left\{1\right\}]=3$.
>So the Galois group is solvable.
>Step 2: Use the subgroup chain
>The theorem tells us that, because the index is the prime $3$ and $K$ contains a primitive cube root of unity, we can enlarge $K$ by adjoining a cube root of some element of $K$.
>In this particular example, we can take $k=2$.
>Therefore we construct $K'=K(\sqrt[3]{2})= \mathbb{Q}(\omega, \sqrt[3]{2})$.
>This is a radical extension because we have simply adjoined the cube root $\sqrt[3]{2}$.
>We already have $a=\sqrt[3]{2}$ in $K'$.
>But we also have $\omega \in K'$, so we automatically get $b=\omega \sqrt[3]{2}$ and $c = \omega^2 \sqrt[3]{2}$.
>Therefore $a,b,c \in K'$.
>So all three roots of the polynomial are now in the field.
>The Galois group has consequently collapsed to $\mathrm{Gal}(f/K')=\left\{1\right\}$.
>The entire problem has been solved by adjoining a single radical.

#### Lemma on Subgroups and Intersections
If $G_i$ is a normal subgroup of $G_{i-1}$ with prime index $p$ and if $\tilde{G}$ is any subgroup of a group containing $G_{i-1}$ then either $G_i \cap \tilde{G}$ is equal to $G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G}$ or it is a normal subgroup of index $p$.

>**Basic idea** 
>If $G_i$ is a normal subgroup of prime index $p$ inside $G_{i-1}$, then when we look at any smaller subgroup $\tilde{G}$, the prime-index step either vanishes or survives unchanged as an index-$p$ normal subgroup. This lemma is useful in the solvability-by-radicals argument because it says that when we pass to an intermediate field (and hence to a subgroup of the Galois group), a prime-sized layer of the original subgroup chain cannot turn into some arbitrary-sized step. It either disappears or remains a prime-sized step.
>
>**Example**
>Consider the group $G_{i-1} = S_3$, the group of all permutations of three objects. It has $6$ elements.
>Let $G_i = A_3$, the subgroup consisting of the even permutations. It has $3$ elements.
>Thus $[G_{i-1}:G_i]=[S_3:A_3]=\frac{6}{3}=2$.
>So $p=2$, which is prime.
>Also, $A_3 \triangleleft S_3$.
>Now let's choose different subgroups $\tilde{G}$ and see what happens.
>
>Case 1: The intersection stays the same
>Take $\tilde{G}=A_3$.
>Then $G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G} = S_3 \cap A_3 = A_3$, and $G_i \cap \tilde{G} = A_3 \cap A_3 = A_3$.
>Therefore $G_i \cap \tilde{G} = G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G}$.
>So in this case, nothing changes.
>
>Case 2: The intersection drops by the prime $p=2$
>Now choose $\tilde{G}=\left\{1, (12) \right\}$.
>This is a subgroup of $S_3$ containing the identity and the transposition $(12)$.
>First consider the intersection with $G_{i-1}=S_3$: $G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G} = S_3 \cap \left\{1,(12) \right\} = \left\{1,(12)\right\}$.
>But $(12)$ is an odd permutation so it does not belong to $A_3$.
>Therefore $G_i \cap \tilde{G} = A_3 \cap \{1,(12) \} = \{1\}$.
>So we have $G_i \cap \tilde{G} = \{ 1 \}$ while $G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G} = \left\{1,(12)\right\}$.
>The index is $[G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G}: G_i \cap \tilde{G}]=[\left\{1,(12)\right\}:{1}]=2$
>And $2=p$.
>Thus $G_i \cap \tilde{G} \triangleleft G_{i-1} \cap \tilde{G}$ with index $2$, exactly as the lemma predicts.
