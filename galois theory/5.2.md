Show that a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ partitions a presentation of $G$ into presentations of $H$ and, in particular, that the number of elements in $H$ divides the number of elements in $G$. (Assume a presentation of $G$ in which no two arrangements are equal, so that the number of arrangements is equal to the number of elements in $G$.)

**Step 1: Construct copies of the presentation of $H$.**
Let the presentation of the subgroup $H$ contain $h_1$, $h_2$, ..., $h_m$.
Pick any element $g \in G$. Multiply every element of $H$ by $g$. This produces $gh_1$, $gh_2$, ..., $gh_m$.
These are all different. For if $gh_i = gh_j$, then multiplying by $g^{-1}$ gives $h_i = h_j$. 
So from the presentation of $H$ we have created another collection of exactly $m$ distinct elements of $G$. It is a copy of the presentation of $H$.

**Step 2: Show that two copies are either disjoint or identical**
Now take another element $k \in G$ and form $kh_1$, $kh_2$, ..., $kh_m$.
Either this new collection has no element in common with the first one, or the two collections are exactly the same. 
Indeed, if $gh_i = kh_j$, then $k=gh_ih_j^{-1}$. Therefore every element of the second collection has the form $kh = gh_ih_j^{-1}h$, where $h_ih_j^{-1}h \in H$ since $H$ is closed under multiplication and inverses. 
Thus, every element of the second collection belongs to the first collection. By the same argument, every element of the first collection belongs to the second collection.
Hence the two collections coincide.

**Step 3: Show that these copies cover $G$.**
Every element $x \in G$ belongs to one of these collections, namely the collection obtained by multiplying $H$ by $x$.
Therefore the elements of $G$ can be grouped into disjoint copies of the presentation of $H$.

**Step 4: Conclude the divisibility result**
Since each copy contains $m = |H|$ elements, and all the copies together contain $|G|$ elements, there exists an integer $r$ such that $|G| = r|H|$.
Hence $|H|$ divides $|G|$.

Notes
- This problem is significant because it is essentially the proof of Lagrange's Theorem, one of the foundational results in finite group theory. 
- This problem reveals the structure of a group. The argument shows that a subgroup $H$ breaks the group $G$ into equal-sized pieces: $gH = \{ gh : h \in H \}$. These pieces are called left cosets of $H$.
- This problems introduces cosets and quotient ideas. The "copies of $H$" constructed in the proof are cosets. Cosets are among the most important concepts in abstract algebra because they lead to: quotient groups, normal subgroups, homomorphisms, the isomorphism theorems, and group actions.
- By "another presentation of $H$", we mean "another arrangement of elements corresponding one-to-one with the elements of $H$". If $g \in H$ then $gH = H$, so the presentation or coset is literally the same set. Otherwise $gH$ is a different subset of $G$ but one that looks exactly like $H$ from the point of view of group structure and cardinality. That's why mathematicians call it a copy of $H$.
