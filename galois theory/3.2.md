Show that in order to construct a 5th root of unity (or an nth root of unity for any $n$) it suffices to find its real part. Give a ruler and compass construction of the real part of one of the 5th roots of unity given in $\S21$. Compare to Euclid's construction of the regular pentagon. (Euclid's Elements. Book 4, Proposition 11. See also Aaboe [A1]. Chapter 2.)

**Show that it suffices to find its real part**
Let $\zeta_n = cos(\frac{2 \pi}{n}) + i sin(\frac{2 \pi}{n})$.
Key observation: $| \zeta_n | = 1$.
So once you know the real part $x = R(\zeta_n) = cos(\frac{2 \pi}{n})$, the imaginary part is forced by the unit-circle equation: $y^2=1-x^2$.
Thus, $\zeta_n = x \pm i \sqrt{1-x^2}$.
Geometrically: 
- construct the point $x$ on the real axis
- draw the unit circle
- erect a perpendicular at $x$; its intersection with the circle gives the roots
Hence constructing an nth root of unity is equivalent to constructing its real part.

**Ruler and compass construction**
For the 5th roots of unity, $\zeta = e^{2 \pi / 5}$, $R(\zeta) = cos(\frac{2 \pi}{5}) = cos \, 72^{\circ}$.
A classical identity gives $cos \, 72^{\circ} = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4}$.
Since unit circle, hypotenus is always 1, so $cos \, 72^{\circ} = x$.
So the problem reduces to constructing $\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4}$ with ruler and compass on the real axis and erecting a perpendicular at that point to find the intersection with the circle, which gives $\zeta = cos \, 72^{\circ} + i \, sin \, 72^{\circ}$ a primitiev 5th root of unity.

**Comparison with Euclid's construction of the regular pentagon**
In Book IV, Proposition 11, Euclid constructs a regular pentagon inscribed in a circle. 
The vertices of a regular pentagon on the unit circle are exactly the 5th roots of unity.
Euclid's construction hinges on dividing a segment in extreme and mean ratio (the golden ratio): $\phi = \frac{1 + \sqrt(5)}{2}$. 

In a regular pentagon, the ratio of a diagonal to a side is $\phi$.
When you inscribe the pentagon in a circle of a radius $1$, the diagonal subtends a central angle of $72^{\circ}$.
Bisecting that isosceles triangle gives a right triangle with angle $36^{\circ}$.
The diagonal length turns out to be $2 \, cos \, 36^{\circ}$.
Since the diagonal-to-side ratio is $\phi$, one gets $2 \, cos \, 36^{\circ} = \phi \Rightarrow cos \, 36^{\circ} = \frac{\phi}{2}$.

Using the double-angle identity: $cos \, 72^{\circ} = 2 \, cos^2 \, 36^{\circ} - 1$ using the double angle identity, substitute $cos \, 36^{\circ} = \phi/2$: $cos \, 72^{\circ} = 2 \, (\frac{\phi}{2})^2 - 1 = \frac{\phi^2}{2} - 1$.
Using a property of the golden ratio $\phi^2 = \phi + 1$, $cos \, 72^{\circ} = \frac{\phi + 1}{2} - 1 = \frac{\phi - 1}{2} = \frac{\sqrt 5 - 1}{4}$.

Euclid is implicitly constructing the same quantity.
