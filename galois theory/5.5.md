Let $f(x)$ be an irreducible cubic equation. Show that the Galois group of $f$ is either the full group of all six substitutions of the roots $a,b,c$ of $f$ or it is the three-element normal subgroup presented in $(2)$ of $\S40$. Show that the latter case occurs if and only if the discriminant of $f$ is a square.

Let $\delta = (a-b)(b-c)(c-a)$, $\Delta = \delta^2 = (a-b)^2(b-c)^2(c-a)^2$ where $\Delta$ is the discriminant of the irreducible cubic $f(x)$.

Since $f$ is irreducible, its Galois group is transitive on the roots $a,b,c$. Hence it contains a cyclic substitution $a \to b$, $b \to c$, $c \to a$ that is , the cyclic substitution $(abc)$.

This substitution acts on the arrangement $abc$ by $abc \mapsto bca$,  $bca \mapsto cab$, $cab \mapsto abc$, so repeated application produces only the three arrangements $abc$, $bca$, $cab$.

These correspond to the three substitutions $1, (abc), (acb)$ which form a group under composition (the three-element group).

Now, any transitive group of substitutions of three letters containing the cyclic substitution must be either this group itself or else must also contain a substitution that interchanges two letters. Once a transposition is present, closure under composition produces all six substitutions of $a,b,c$. Hence, the only possibilities are the three-element group above or the full group of six substitutions. Therefore, the Galois group is one of these two groups.

Now consider $\delta$. A cyclic substitution merely permutes the factors $(a-b), (b-c), (c-a)$ so it leaves $\delta$ unchanged. An interchange of two letters changes the sign of exactly one factor, hence sends $\delta$ to $-\delta$. Therefore the three substitutions in the above group fix $\delta$, while the other three change its sign. 

If the Galois group is the three-element group, then every substitution in the group fixes $\delta$, so $\delta$ lies in the rationality domain (i.e., $\delta \in F$ where $F = \{ x \in K | \sigma(x) = x$ for all $\sigma \in G \}$ is a field ). Hence $\Delta = \delta^2$ is a square in the rationality domain. 

Conversely, suppose the discriminant is a square in the rationality domain, so $\Delta = d^2$ for some $d \in F$. Then $\delta^2 = d^2 \Rightarrow (\delta - d)(\delta + d) = 0$, so in the splitting field, $\delta = \pm d$. This tells us that $\delta$ is equal to an element of $F$, up to a sign choice. But note: $d \in F$ and $-d \in F$ so in either case: $\delta \in F$ (i.e, $\delta$ itself lies in the rationality domain). 

Since every substitution in the Galois group leaves $\delta$ unchanged, we have $\sigma(\delta) = \delta$ for all $\sigma \in G$. Hence $\delta$ is fixed by all substitutions of the Galois group. But any substitution outside the three-elements group changes $\delta$ to $-\delta$, so no such substitution can lie in the Galois group. Hence the Galois group is exactly the three-element group.

Therefore the Galois group of an irreducible cubic is either the full group of six substitutions of the roots or the three-element normal subgroup and the latter occurs if and only if the discriminant is a square in the rationality domain.

Notes
- This problem classifies all irreducible cubics in one stroke. The result says: either the Galois group is $S_3$ (all $6$ permtutations), or it is $A_3$, the cyclic group of order $3$ (the even permutations) and there are no other possibilities.
- This problem explains solvability by radicals in the cubic case. Even though cubics are always solvable by radicals, this result tells you: when the structure is "simpler" (cyclic group $A_3$) vs when it is "maximally complicated" for degree 3 $(S_3)$ so it separates: "pure cyclic behavior" (like $x^3 - 2$) from "full permutation behavior" (like most cubics).
- This problem presents an example where a single invariant—the discriminant—distinguishes the two possible Galois groups of an irreducible cubic ($S_3$​ vs $A_3$​) by detecting whether odd permutations occur. More generally, for a separable polynomial over a field, its splitting field has a Galois group acting as permutations of its roots, and certain invariants (such as the discriminant) detect specific structural properties of this permutation action, such as whether odd permutations occur.
- A transposition is a substitution that swaps two roots and leaves the third fixed, for example: $(ab): a \leftrightarrow b, c$ fixed.
- Key structural fact is: if your group contains a $3-$ cycle and also contains any transposition, then it forces all $6$ permutations.
- Each substitution in the Galois group is an automorphism of the splitting field $K$ that: permutes the roots $a,b,c$ and fixes every element of the base field $F$ so every element of the Galois group fixes the rationality domain pointwise.
- In the latter part of the problem, we are proving $Gal(f) = \{1, (abc),(acb)\} \iff \Delta$ is a square in $F$. 
- Subgroup presented in $(2)$ of $\S40$ is $abc, bca, cab$ and $cba, bac, acb$.
