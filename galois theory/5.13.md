Let $H = \left\{1, (ab)(cd), (ac)(bd), (ad)(bc) \right\}$, the subgroup presented by the first of Galois' presentations in $(3) of \S40$ which is as above.
To show that $H$ is normal, it suffices (by Galois' criterion) to show that for every substitution $T$ of the group and every $S \in H$,  $T^{-1}ST \in H$. 
The identity remains fixed under conjugation. Each of the other three substitutions of $H$ interchanges two disjoint pairs of letters. If substitution $S = (xy)(zw)$, then $T^{-1}ST = (T^{-1}(x)T^{-1}(y))(T^{-1}(z)T^{-1}(w))$.  So $T^{-1}ST$ again interchanges two disjoint pairs of letters.
But on the four letters $a,b,c,d$ there are only three such substitutions, $(ab)(cd), (ac)(bd), (ad)(bc)$ which are precisely the three nonidentity elements of $H$.
Hence $T^{-1}ST \in H$ for every $S \in H$.
Therefore $T^{-1}HT = H$, so $H$ is a normal subgroup.

Notes
- This problem shows how normal subgroups are subgroups whose structure is preserved under the whole group.
- A subgroup is called a normal subgroup if and only if it is true that, for any $T$ in the group, and any $S$ in the subgroup, the substitution $T^{-1}ST$ is in the subgroup. 
- Let $S = (ab)(cd)$ and let $T=(abc)$. Then $T(a)=b$, $T(b)=c$, $T(c)=a$, $T(d)=d$. To compute $T^{-1}ST$, we see what it does to each letter. For example $a \xrightarrow{T} b \xrightarrow{S} a \xrightarrow{T^{-1}} c$ . Thus, $T^{-1}(ab)(cd)T = (ac)(bd)$, another double transposition. We see that $(ac)(bd)$ is the same as $(T^{-1}(b)T^{-1}(a))(T^{-1}(c)T^{-1}(d)) = (T^{-1}(a)T^{-1}(b))(T^{-1}(c)T^{-1}(d))$.
