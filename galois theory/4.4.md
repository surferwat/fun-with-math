Prove that a product of a nonzero polynomial is nonzero.

Let $R$ be an integral domain (in Abstract Algebra, this means a commutative ring with no zero divisions). Let $f(x), g(x) \in \mathbb{R}[x]$ be  nonzero polynomials. We will prove that their product $f(x)g(x)$ is also nonzero.

Write $f(x) = a_mx^m + ... + a_0, a_m \ne 0$, $g(x) = b_nx^n + ... + b_0$, $b_n \ne 0$.
Then the product is $f(x)g(x) = c_{m+n}x^{m+n} + ... + c_0$, where the leading coefficient is $c_{m+n} = a_mb_n$.
Since $\mathbb{R}$ is an integral domain and $a_m \ne 0$, $b_n \ne 0$, we have $a_mb_n \ne 0$.
Thus the coefficient $c_{m+n}$ is nonzero, so $f(x)g(x)$ has a nonzero leading term. 
Therefore, $f(x)g(x) \ne 0$.

Notes
- A ring is a set where you can: add and multiply; subtraction works; multiplication distributes over addition. Commutative means multiplication doesn't depend on order $ab=ba$. A zero divisor is a nonzero element that multiplies with another nonzero element to give zero. That is $a\ne0$, $b\ne0$ but $ab=0$. If this ever happens in a ring, it causes trouble - especially for arguments like the one in your Galois theory problem. Thus a commutative ring with no zero divisors (an integral domain) is a system where: multiplication is commutative, and the only way a product is zero is if one of the factors was already zero.
- In Harold's argument, the giant expression $\mathcal{D} = \prod_{S \ne T}[...]^2$ is built as a product of many polynomial factors. The goal is to show: $\mathcal{D} \ne 0$ as a polynomial; therefore, you can choose values of $A, B, C$... so that $\mathcal{D} \ne 0$ numerically; which guarantees all those permutation expressions are distinct. This entire chain collapses if a product of nonzero polynomials could somehow become zero. So this "small" fact is what makes the whole construction of a Galois resolvent actually work.
