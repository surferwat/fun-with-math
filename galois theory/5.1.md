Show that a set of substitutions $n$ objects $a,b,c,...$ is a group iff it is closed under composition.

Let $S$ be a nonempty set of substitutions of $n$ objects that is closed under composition.
We must show that $S$ is a group.

**Step 1: Associativity**
Composition of substitutions is associative. 
Therefore, $(\sigma \tau) \rho = \sigma(\tau \rho)$ for all $\sigma, \tau, \rho \in S$.
So the associativity axiom is already satisfied.

**Step 2: Show that the identity substitution belongs to $S$.**
Choose any substitution $\sigma \in S$.
Since $S$ is finite, the powers $\sigma, \sigma^2, \sigma^3,...$ cannot all be different.
Hence there exist integers $r > s \ge 1$ s.t. $\sigma^r = \sigma^s$.
Since $\sigma$ is a permutation, it has an inverse in the symmetric group. From $\sigma^r = \sigma^s$, multiplying both sides on the left by $\sigma^{-s}$ yields $\sigma^{r-s} = e$.
Cancelling $s$ copies of $\sigma$ gives $\sigma^{r-s} = e$, where $e$ is the identity substitution.
But $\sigma^{r-s}$ is obtained by composing elements of $S$, so closure implies $e \in S$.

**Step 3: Show that every element has an inverse in $S$**
Let $\sigma \in S$.
From Step 2 we know that $\sigma^k = e$ for some positive integer $k$.
Then $\sigma \cdot \sigma^{k-1} = \sigma^k = e$, and likewise $\sigma^{k-1} \cdot \sigma = e$.
Thus $\sigma^{k-1}$ is the inverse of $\sigma$.
Since $\sigma^{k-1}$ is a power of $\sigma$, closure implies $\sigma^{k-1} \in S$.
Therefore every element of $S$ has its inverse in $S$.

**Conclusion**
We have shown that $S$ (1) is closed under composition (assumption), (2) is associative, (3) contains the identity, and (4) contains inverses of all its elements.
Therefore, $S$ is a group.
Conversely, every group is closed under composition by definitino.
Hence a nonempty set of substitutions of $n$ objects is a group if and only if it is closed under composition.
A nonempty set of substitutions of $n$ objects is a group iff it is closed under composition.

Notes
- In relation to Step 2, suppose we know that $\sigma^r = \sigma^s$ with $r > s$. Write this as $\sigma^s \sigma^{r-s} = \sigma^s$. Since $\sigma$ is a permutation, it has an inverse $\sigma^{-1}$ in the full symmetric group on the $n$ objects. Multiplying both sides on the left by $(\sigma^{-1})^s$ gives $\sigma^{r-s} = e$. This is what is meant by "cancelling $s$ copies of $\sigma$."
- Normally, to prove that a set is a group, you must verify four axioms: 1) closure, 2) associativity, 3) identity, 4) inverses. For a set of permutations, this theorem says: closure alone is enough (assuming the set is nonempty). The other axioms come for free. This is one of the first examples of a subgroup criterion: instead of checking all the group axioms, you can often check a much smaller condition. 
- In Galois theory, one studies sets of permutations of roots of a polynomial. For example, if a polynomial has roots $a,b,c,d,$ one looks at substitutions of these roots that preserve algebraic relations among them. A central task is "show that these substitutions form a group". This theorem makes that much easier. Instead of checking identity and inverses explicitly, you only need to show: composing two admissible substitutions gives another admissible substitution. Then the theorem guarantees you have a group.
- For mathematicians like Galois, a "group" was not initially an abstract set with axioms. It was literally a collection of permutations of roots of equations. Results like this showed that permutation groups are much more rigid than arbitrary algebraic systems. Closure almost forces the whole group structure.
