Use Galois' method of $\S37$ to express the roots $a,b,c$ of an irreducible cubic $x^3 + px +q=0$ as a rational functions of $t=a-b$. Of the three, $c$ has the simplest expression. Verify that if $t$ is a root of the equation found in Exercise 8 then this rational function $c$ of $t$ is a root of $x^3 +px + q =0$.

Let $f(x) = x^3 + px + q$ have roots $a,b,c$ and set $t=a-b$. 
Since the coefficient of $x^2$ is $0$, Viet's formula says $a+b+c=0$.
Hence $a+b=-c$.
Now we know both the sum and difference of $a$ and $b$: $a+b=-c$, $a-b=t$.
Solving the linear system of equations, $a= \frac{-c+t}{2}$, $b= \frac{-c-t}{2}$. (1)
Thus, once we express $c$ rationally in terms of $t$, the same will be true for $a$ and $b$.

**Finding $c$**
Use the relation from Viet's formula, $ab+ac+bc=p$.
From (1), $ab=\frac{(-c+t)(-c-t)}{4} = \frac{c^2-t^2}{4}$.
Also, $ac+bc = c(a+b) = -c^2$.
Therefore, $p=\frac{c^2-t^2}{4}-c^2$, so $4p=c^2-t^2-4c^2 = -3c^2-t^2$.
Hence $3c^2+t^2=-4p$. (2)
Now use $abc=-q$.
Since $ab=\frac{c^2-t^2}{4}$, we get $c(c^2-t^2)=-4q$.
Using (2), $c^2 = -\frac{t^2+4p}{3}$, so $c(-\frac{t^2+4p}{3}-t^2)=-4q$.
Simplifying, $c(-\frac{4(t^2+p)}{3}) = -4q$, and therefore $c=\frac{3q}{t^2+p}$.
Substituting into (1), 
$a = \frac{1}{2}(t-\frac{3q}{t^2+p})$,
$b = -\frac{1}{2}(t+\frac{3q}{t^2+p})$.
Thus, all three roots are rational functions of $t=a-b$.

**Equation satisfied by $t$.**
From $c^2 = -\frac{t^2+4p}{3}$ and  $c=\frac{3q}{t^2+p}$, we obtain $\frac{9q^2}{(t^2+p)^2} = -\frac{t^2+4p}{3}$.
Multiplying through, $(t^2+4p)(t^2+p)^2+27q^2 = 0$.
Equivalently, $t^6+6pt^4+9p^2t^2+4p^3+27q^2=0$.

**Verification**
Assume $t$ satisfies $(t^2+4p)(t^2+p)^2 + 27q^2 = 0$.
and define $c= \frac{3q}{t^2+p}$.
Then $c^3 + pc + q = \frac{27q^3}{(t^2+p)^3} + \frac{3pq}{t^2+p} + q$.
Combining terms, $c^3 + pc + q = \frac{q}{(t^2+p)^3}[27q^2+(t^2+p)^2(t^2+4p)]$.
The bracket is $0$ by the equation satisfied by $t$. Hence $c^3 + pc + q = 0$.
Therefore $c=\frac{3q}{t^2+p}$ is indeed a root of $x^3 + px + q$.

Notes
- The significance of this problem is that it illustrates the core idea of Galois theory: instead of solving for roots directly, we study structured combinations of roots that reflect their symmetry. By introducing $t=a-b$, we reduce the three roots $a,b,c$ of the cubic to a single auxiliary quantity, from which all roots can be recovered as rational functions. The computation shows that $t$ itself satisfies a higher-degree algebraic equation, while also allowing one root (notably $c$) to be expressed very simply in terms of $t$. This demonstrates how the structure of the roots is encoded in symmetric and antisymmetric expressions, revealing that solving a polynomial is fundamentally about understanding how its roots related under permutation rather than computing them directly.
- The structure of the solution is 
	- Use $a+b+c = 0$, $t=a-b$ to express $a,b$ in terms of $c,t$.
	- Use the elementary symmetric relations to solve for $c$ rationally in $t$.
	- Derive the sextic equation for $t$.
	- Verify directly that the obtained formula for $c$ satisfies the cubic.
- We it's important to find an equation satisfied by $t$. This is the key Galois-theory idea: we started with roots $a,b,c$, we introduced a simpler quantity $t=a-b$, we expressed everything in terms of $t$, now we find the equation that $t$ must satisfy. So instead of solving a cubic in $x$, we reduce the problem to studying the algebraic equation satisfied by a symmetric combination of roots. This step shows $t$ is not arbitrary - it is itself an algebraic element over $\mathbb{Q}(p,q)$, satisfiying a degree-$6$ equation. That equation encodes how the original cubic "splits" in terms of differences of roots, which is exactly what Galois' method is designed to expose.

Book excerpt
The main tool in the above expression of $K(a,b,c,...)$ in the simple form $K(t)$ is Lagrange's theorem of $\S29$ which is used to show that the roots $a,b,c, ...$ can all be expressed as polynomials in the Galois resovelent $t$. This, however, is not the approach that Galois himself took. He mentioned neither Lagrange nor Lagrange's theorem and instead stated and proved the crucial fact as follows:

Lemma 111. When the function* $t$ has been chosen as above [that is, so that it has $n!$ different values when the roots are permuted], it has the property that all the roots of the equation can be expressed rationally in terms of $t$.

"In fact, let $t = \phi(a,b,c,...)$, which is to say $t-\phi(a,b,c,...)=0$. Let us multiply all of the similar equations which one obtains in permuting the roots, keeping only the first one fixed. This gives $(t-\phi(a,b,c,d,...))(t-\phi(a,c,b,d,...))(t-\phi(a,b,d,c,...))...$", which is symmetric in $b,c,d, ...$ and consequently can be written as a function of $a$. We will then have an equation of the form $F(t,a)=0$. But I say that one can solve this for $a$. For this it suffices to seek the common solution of this equation and the given one. This solution, that is $a$, is the only one they have in common, because one cannot have, for example, $F(t,b) = 0$ without the consequence (this equation having a common factor with the similar equation) that one of the function $\phi(a,...)$ was equal to one of the function $\phi(b,...)$, which is contrary to the hypothesis. From this it follows that $a$ can be expressed as a rational function of $t$, and it is the same for the other roots." (See Appendix 1.)

Let $F(X,Y)$ be the polynomial obtained from the product of the $(n-1)!$ expressions $X - \phi(Y,b,c,d,...)$ when the roots $b, c, d, ...$ are permuted in all possible ways, when the symmetric polynomials in $b,c,d,...$ are expressed in terms of $a$ (see the end of $\S30$), and when $a$ is replaced by $Y$. Then $F(t,a) =0$, as Galois says, and what is to be shown is that this equation can be solved for $a$ in terms of $t$. Let $f$ be the given polynomial with roots $a,b,c,...$ Let the Euclidean algorithm be used to find the greatest common divisor of $f(Y)$ and $F(t,y)$ when both are regarded as polynomials in $Y$ with coefficients in the field $K(t)$. This gives $d(Y) = A(Y)F(t,Y) + B(Y)f(Y)$ where $d$, $A$, and $B$ are polynomials with coefficients in $K(t)$ and $d(Y)$ is a divisor of both $F(t,Y)$ and $f(Y)$. Since $Y-a$ divides both $F(t,Y)$ and $f(Y)$, it divides $d(Y)$. Galois contends that $d(Y)$ has no other factors. Since $f(Y)$ is a constant times $(Y-a)(Y-b)(Y-c) ... d(Y)$ is a (nonzero) constant times $Y-a$ times a product of some subset of the factors $Y-b, Y-c, ...$ If $Y-b$ divides $d(Y)$ then it divides $F(t,Y)$, that is $F(t,b)=0$. To prove Galois' contention that $d(Y) = \gamma(Y-a)(\gamma \in K(t), \gamma \ne 0)$ it will suffice to prove that $F(t,b) \ne 0$ and by symmetry $F(t,c) \ne 0$,...

The expressions of the elementary symmetric functions of $n-1$ of the roots in terms of the remaining root - namely $\tau_1 = \sigma_1 - a, \tau_2 = \sigma_2 - a \sigma_1 + a^2$, $\tau_3 = \sigma_3 - a\sigma_2 + a^2\sigma_1 - a^3,...$ - are independent of the choice of the special root. Therefore $F(X,Y)$ can also be described as the product of all $(n-1)!$ expressions $X-\phi(Y,a,c,d,...)$ when the roots $a,c,d,...$ are permuted in all possible ways, when the symmetric polynomials in $a,c,d,...$ are expressed in terms of $b$, and when $b$ is replaced by $Y$. This shows that $F(t,b)$ is equal to the product of the $(n-1)!$ terms $t-\phi(b,a,c,...) = \phi(a,b,c,...)-\phi(b,a,c,...)$ Since by assumption these factors are not zero, this shows that $F(t,b) \ne 0$ and consequently that $d(Y) = \gamma(Y-a)(\gamma \in K(t), \gamma \ne 0)$. 

Setting $Y = 0$ in $d(Y) = A(Y)F(t,Y) + B(Y)f(Y)$ gives $\gamma \cdot (-a) \in K(t)$. Then division by $-\gamma$ gives $a \in K(t)$, as was to be shown. (See Exercise 9 for an application of Galois' method in a specific case.)
