A group is called cyclic if it contains a substitution $S$ with the property that every element of the group is a power of $S$. For example, the groups presented in $(1)$ and $(2)$ of $\S42$ are cyclic. Show that every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Let $G$ be a cyclic group, so that $G = \langle S \rangle = \left\{ S^n : n \in \mathbb{Z} \right\}$ for some element $S \in G$. 
Let $H \le G$. 
We will show that $H$ is cyclic.

If $H = \left\{ e \right\}$ then $H = \langle e \rangle$, so $H$ is cyclic.

Now suppose $H \ne \left\{ e \right\}$. Since every element of $G$ is a power of $S$, every element of $H$ is also a power of $S$.
Define $A = \left\{ n \in \mathbb{N} : S^n \in H \right\}$.
The set $A$ is nonempty. Indeed, choose $h \in H \setminus \left\{ e \right\}$.
Since $G = \langle S \rangle$, we have $h = S^m$ for some integer $m$. 
If $m > 0$, then $m \in A$. If $m < 0$ then $h^{-1} = S^{-m} \in H$, so $-m \in A$.
By the Well-Ordering Principle, $A$ has a least element; call it $d$. 
We claim that $H = \langle S^d \rangle$.
Since $S^d \in H$ and $H$ is a subgroup of $G$, every power of $S^d$ belongs to $H$. 
Thus $\langle S^d \rangle \subseteq H$.

Conversely, let $S^k \in H$. 
By the Division Algorithm, $k = qd + r$, $0 \le r < d$.
Then $S^r = S^k(S^d)^{-q}$.
Since both $S^k$ and $(S^d)^{-q}$ lie in $H$, we have $S^r \in H$.
If $r > 0$, then $r \in A$, contradicting the minimality of $d$. 
Hence $r = 0$, so $k = qd$. 
Therefore $S^k = (S^d)^q \in \langle S^d \rangle$.
Thus every element of $H$ belongs to $\langle S^d \rangle$, and so $H \subseteq \langle S^d \rangle$.

Combining the two inclusions, $H = \langle S^d \rangle$.
Hence $H$ is cyclic.

Notes
- $\langle S \rangle$ means the subgroup generated by $S$.
