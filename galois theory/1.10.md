Use the resultant to reduce the solution of two equations in two variables f(x,y)=0, g(x,y)=0 to the solution of polynomials in one variable in the case where f and g both have degree 3 at most in x. In all likelihood, this is the application that Newton had in mind and it is the reason he did not assume a=A=1. (Rather rough arguments are all one can expect here, since the notion of a solution of an equation has yet to be spelled out.)

Let $f(x,y)= a_0(y)x^3 + a_1(y)x^2 + a_2(y)x + a_3(y)$
Let $g(x,y)= b_0(y)x^3 + b_1(y)x^2 + b_2(y)x + b_3(y)$

$R(a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,b_0,b_1,b_2,b_3)=$
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\quad -b_0(y)^3 a_3(y)^3 + b_0(y)^2 b_1(y) a_2(y) a_3(y)^2 - b_0(y) b_1(y)^2 a_1(y) a_3(y)^2 + b_1(y)^3 a_0(y) a_3(y)^2 \\
&\quad - b_0(y)^2 b_2(y) a_3(y) a_2(y)^2 + 2b_0(y)^2 b_2(y) a_1(y) a_3(y)^2 + b_0(y) b_1(y) b_2(y) a_1(y) a_2(y) a_3(y) \\
&\quad - 3b_0(y) b_1(y) b_2(y) a_0(y) a_3(y)^2 - b_1(y)^2 b_2(y) a_0(y) a_2(y) a_3(y) \\
&\quad - b_0(y) b_2(y)^2 a_1(y)^2 a_3(y) + 2b_0(y) b_2(y)^2 a_0(y) a_2(y) a_3(y) + b_1(y) b_2(y)^2 a_0(y) a_1(y) a_3(y) \\
&\quad - b_2(y)^3 a_0(y)^2 a_3(y) + b_0(y)^2 b_3(y) a_2(y)^3 - 3b_0(y)^2 b_3(y) a_1(y) a_2(y) a_3(y) + 3b_0(y)^2 b_3(y) a_0(y) a_3(y)^2 \\
&\quad - b_0(y) b_1(y) b_3(y) a_1(y) a_2(y)^2 + 2b_0(y) b_1(y) b_3(y) a_1(y)^2 a_3(y) + b_0(y) b_1(y) b_3(y) a_0(y) a_2(y) a_3(y) \\
&\quad + b_1(y)^2 b_3(y) a_0(y) a_2(y)^2 - 2b_1(y)^2 b_3(y) a_0(y) a_1(y) a_3(y) + b_0(y) b_2(y) b_3(y) a_1(y)^2 a_2(y) \\
&\quad - 2b_0(y) b_2(y) b_3(y) a_0(y) a_2(y)^2 - b_0(y) b_2(y) b_3(y) a_0(y) a_1(y) a_3(y) \\
&\quad - b_1(y) b_2(y) b_3(y) a_0(y) a_1(y) a_2(y) + 3b_1(y) b_2(y) b_3(y) a_0(y)^2 a_3(y) \\
&\quad + b_2(y)^2 b_3(y) a_0(y)^2 a_2(y) - b_0(y) b_3(y)^2 a_1(y)^3 + 3b_0(y) b_3(y)^2 a_0(y) a_1(y) a_2(y) \\
&\quad - 3b_0(y) b_3(y)^2 a_0(y)^2 a_3(y) + b_1(y) b_3(y)^2 a_0(y) a_1(y)^2 - 2b_1(y) b_3(y)^2 a_0(y)^2 a_2(y) \\
&\quad - b_2(y) b_3(y)^2 a_0(y)^2 a_1(y) + b_3(y)^3 a_0(y)^3
\end{aligned}
$$

Notes (from answer key in back of textbook)
- Here $a,b,c,d,A,B,C,D$ are polynomials in $y$ so the resultant $P$ is a polynomial in $y$. If $(x',y')$ is a root of both equations then $x'$ is a root of both $f(x,y')$ and $g(x,y')$ which shows that $P(y')=0$ unless $A(y')=a(y')=0$. This gives a finite set of values of $y'$ unless $A=a \equiv 0$ or $P \equiv 0$. For each of the $y'$ in this finite set, solve the equations $f(x,y')=0$, $g(x,y')$ to find all common solutions $x'$. 
- If $A=a \equiv 0$ then $f$ and $g$ are quadratic at most in $x$ and a similar procedure can be followed using the resultant of two 2nd degree equations.
- If $P \equiv 0$ without $A \equiv 0$ then there is at least one value of $x'$ for each value of $y$, hence $\infty$ solutions. In this case it is to be expected that $f$ and $g$ have a common factor $h(x,y)$.
- "... it is the reason he did not assume $a=A=1$". This is a historical comment about why Newton kept general leading coefficients rather than simplifying by dividing them to $1$ (as we often do in modern algebra). Why didn't Newton just assume leading coefficient = 1?  Because resultants are sensitive to leading coefficients - if you divide $f$ and $g$ by $a_0(y)$, $b_0(y)$, you might introduce denominators, and the resultant would no longer be a polynomial in $y$ - which defeats the whole purpose. Newton likely understood that to maintain the resultant as a polynomial (which is critical for finding roots!), the equations should be kept in their general form.
