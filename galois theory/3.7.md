Prove Lemma 1 of $\S 24$ which states that every prime has a primitive root.

Lemma 1: For every prime $p$ there is an integer $g$ with the property that every integer not congruent to $0$ mod $p$ is congruent to a power of $g$ mod $p$. Such an integer $g$ is called a primitive root mod $p$. 

$n \equiv 0 \, mod \, p \Rightarrow p | n - 0$ 
Example
Let $p = 3$.
Then $n=3,6,9,...$
And $1,2,4,5,7,8,10,11 ...$ are integers not congruent to $0 \, mod \, p$.
These integers are congruent to a power of $g \, mod \, p$.
Let $g = 2$.
$1 \equiv 2^2 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$2 \equiv 2^1 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$4 \equiv 2^2 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$5 \equiv 2^1 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$7 \equiv 2^2 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$8 \equiv 2^1 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$10 \equiv 2^2 \, mod \, 3$. ok
$11 \equiv 2^1 \, mod \, 3$. ok
 and so on

Let $p$ be a prime. 
Since $p$ is prime, the integers not divisible by $p$ form a reduced residue system $1,2,$...,$p-1$ modulo $p$. 
By Fermat's theorem, for every integer $a$ not divisible by $p$, $a^{p-1} \equiv 1, (mod \, p)$.
Thus, every such integer satisfies the congruence $x^{p-1} \equiv \, (mod \, p)$.

**Step 1: Number of solutions of polynomial congruences**
Since arithmetic modulo $p$ forms a field, a polynomial congruence of degree $n$ modulo $p$ can have at most $n$ solutions.
Therefore, for any divisor $d$ of $p-1$, the congruence $x^d \equiv (mod \, p)$ has at most $d$ solutions.

**Step 2: Prime factorization of $p-1$**
Let $p-1 = q_1^{e_1} q_2^{e_2} ... q_r^{e_r}$ be the factorization of $p-1$ into prime powers.
We shall prove that there exists an integer whose order modulo $p$ is exactly $p-1$.

**Step 3: Contradiction argument**
Suppose no integer has order $p-1$ modulo $p$.
Then for every integer $a$ not divisible by $p$, the smallest exponent $d$ s.t. $a^d \equiv 1 (mod \, p)$ is strictly less than $p-1$.
Hence this exponent must divide one of the numbers $\frac{p-1}{q_1}$, $\frac{p-1}{q_2}$, ..., $\frac{p-1}{q_r}$.
Thus every integer not divisible by $p$ would satisfy $x^{(p-1)/q_i} \equiv 1 \, (mod \, p)$ for at least one index $i$. 
That is, every nonzero residue mod $p$ would be a root of at least one of the congruences $x^{(p-1/q_i)} - 1 \equiv 0$ (mod $p$).

**Step 4: Counting roots**
Each congruence $x^{(p-1)/q_i} \equiv 1$ (mod \, $p$) has at most $\frac{p-1}{q_i}$ solutions.
Hence the total number of integers satisfying at least one of these congruences is at most $\sum_{i=1}^r \frac{p-1}{q_i}$.
But since each $q_i \ge 2$, we have $\sum_{i=1}^r \frac{p-1}{q_i} < p-1$.
Thus fewer than $p-1$ integers can satisfy these congruences.
This contradicts the assumption that all nonzero residues mod $p$ do so.

**Step 5: Conclusion**
Therefore there must exist at least one integer $g$ s.t. that the smallest exponent for which $g^{p-1} \equiv 1$ (mod $p$) is exactly $p-1$.
The powers $1, g,, g^2, ..., g^{p-2}$ are therefore all incongruent module $p$, and since there are $p-1$ of them, they form a complete system of nonzero residues mod $p$.
Hence every nonzero residue modulo $p$ is congruent to a power of $g$.
Every prime $p$ has a primitive root.

Notes
- Without primitive roots, the multiplicative structure mod $p$ would be messy.
- With a primitive root $g$: every nonzero residue mod $p$ becomes $1,g,g^2,g^3,..., g^{p-2}$ so multiplication mod $p$ becomes: $g^a \cdot g^b = g^{a+b}$. Multiplication turns into addition of exponents. This dramatically simplifies: solving congruences, understanding powers mod $p$, studying orders of elements, proving results like quadratic reciprocity, and understanding discrete logarithms
