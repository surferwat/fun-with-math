Do the same for the twenty-four element group of all substitutions of four letters.

Suppose the quartic has roots $a,b,c,d$.
Initially, we know only the coefficients, so every permutation of the roots is possible. 
The permutation group is $G_{24}$.
The idea is to successively introduce quantities that distinguish between more and more permutations.
Each new quantity is invariant under a smaller subgroup, but not under the next large subgroup.
Knowing that quantity shrinks the possible permutation group.

First step: $G_{24} \to G_{12}$
Initially, every permutation of the four roots is possible, so the group of substitutions is 
$\begin{aligned} G_{24}=\{& e,\\ &(ab),(ac),(ad),(bc),(bd),(cd),\\ &(ab)(cd),(ac)(bd),(ad)(bc),\\ &(abc),(acb),(abd),(adb),(acd),(adc),(bcd),(bdc),\\ &(abcd),(abdc),(acbd),(acdb),(adbc),(adcb) \}. \end{aligned}$
We now seek a quantity whose behavior distinguishes between even and odd substitutions. The natural choice is the Vandermonde determinant: $\Delta = (a-b)(a-c)(a-d)(b-c)(b-d)(c-d)$.
This quantity is chosen because a permutation of the roots either preserves or reverses the order of the factors. An even permutation leaves the produce unchanged, while an odd permutation reverses its sign.
For example, the even permutation $(a,b,c,d) \mapsto (b,c,a,d)$ gives $\Delta \mapsto \Delta$, whereas the transposition $(a,b,c,d) \mapsto (b,a,c,d)$ gives $\Delta \mapsto -\Delta$. 
Therefore once $\Delta$ is adjoined, a substitution is allowed only if it leaves $\Delta$ fixed. The odd substitutions are eliminated, leaving only the even substitutions: $G_{24} \to G_{12}$.
The remaining question is how to obtain obtain $\Delta$. We cannot express $\Delta$ directly in terms of the coefficients of the quartic, because it is not symmetric. However, $\Delta^2$ is symmetric: the sign change under odd permutations disappears after squaring.
Therefore $\Delta^2$ can be expressed in terms of the elementary symmetric functions of the roots, and hence in terms of the coefficients of the quartic.
Thus, $\Delta = \pm \sqrt{\text{known expression}}$.
The first radical is a square root.
Adjoining the square root reduces the possible substitutions from all permutations of the roots to only even substitutions.

Second step: $G_{12} \to G_4$
After adjoining the Vandermonde determinant, only the even substutions remain possible: $G_12 = \left\{e, (ab)(cd), (ac)(bd), (ad)(bc), (abc), (acb),(abd),(adb),(acd),(adc),(bcd),(bdc) \right\}$.
We now seek a quantity that reduces this group further. The natural objects to consider are the three ways of dividing four roots into two pairs: $(ab)(cd)$, $(ac)(bd)$, $(ad)(bc)$.
Associate to these pairings the quantities $P= ab+cd$, $Q=ac+bd$, and $R=ad+bc$.
These quantities are chosen because they reflect the three possible pairings of the roots. The group $G_{12}$ does not leave $P,Q,R$ individually fixed; instead, it permutes them. 
For example, a suitable $3-$cycle of the roots gives $P \mapsto Q$, $Q \mapsto R$, and $R \mapsto P$.
Thus the remaining symmetry acts by permuting the three quantities $P,Q,R$. To reduce this symmetry, we must determine the individual values of $P,Q,R$.
The natural object is therefore the polynomial having $P,Q,R$ as its roots: $(x-P)(x-Q)(x-R)$.
Expanding gives $x^3 - (P+Q+R)x^2 + (PQ + QR+RP)x - PQR$.
The coefficients of this cubic are symmetric functions of $P,Q,R$. Since $P,Q,R$ are themselves built from $a,b,c,d$ these coefficients can be expressed in terms of the elementary symmetric functions $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3, \sigma_4$ of the original roots, and hence in terms of the coefficients of the quartic.  For example, $P+Q+R = ab+ac+ad+bc+bd+cd = \sigma_2$.
Similarly, $PQ+QR+RP$ and $PQR$ can be expressed in terms of the $\sigma_i$. Therefore we obtain a cubic equation $(x-P)(x-Q)(x-R) = 0$ whose coefficients are already known. This is the cubic resolvent.
To solve this cubic, we use Lagrange's resolvent. Introduce $U =P+\alpha Q + \alpha^2R$, where $\alpha^3 = 1,$ $1+\alpha+\alpha^2=0$.
This choice is made because it converts the cyclic permutation of $P,Q,R$ into multiplication by a constant.
Under the cycle: $(P,Q,R) \mapsto (Q,R,P)$, we have $U \mapsto Q + \alpha R + \alpha^2 P$.
Factoring out $\alpha^2$, $Q + \alpha R + \alpha^2 P = alpha^2(P+\alpha Q+\alpha^2 R)$.
So, $U \mapsto \alpha^2 U$.
Therefore, $U^3 \mapsto (\alpha^2)^3 U^3 = U^3$.
Hence $U^3$ is invariant under the cyclic permutation of $P, Q, R$. 
Thus, it can be determined from the coefficients of the cubic resolvent.
Therefore $U= \sqrt[3]{\text{known expression}}$.
This introduces the second radical: a cube root.
To recover $P,Q,R$ introduce the conjugate resolvent,  $V = P+\alpha^2 Q + \alpha R$. 
Together with $S = P+Q+R$, we have $S = P+Q+R$, $U = P + \alpha Q + \alpha^2 R$, $V =  P + \alpha^2 Q + \alpha R$. 
Adding these equations, $S+U+V = 3P + (1+\alpha+\alpha^2)Q + (1+\alpha+\alpha^2)R$.
Since $1+\alpha+\alpha^2=0$, the $Q$ and $R$ terms disappear: $S+U+V=3P$.
Therefore, $P = \frac{S+U+V}{3}$.
The same construction gives $Q$ and $R$.
Once the values of $P,Q,R$ are known, any substitution that permutes them is no longer possible. The remaining substitutions must leave each of $P,Q,R$ fixed. These are precisely the double swaps: $G_4 = \left\{e, (ab)(cd), (ac)(bd),(ad)(bc)\right\}$.
Thus the cubic resolvent reduces the group: $G_{12} \to G_4$.

Third step: $G_4 \to G_2$
After solving the cubic resolvent, the remaining substitutions are $G_4 = \left\{e,(ab)(cd)(ac)(bd),(ad)(bc)\right\}$.
These are the three possible double swaps of the roots. We now see a quantity that distinguishes among these remaining substitutions.
The quantities already known, $P= ab+cd$, $Q = ac+bd$, $R = ad +bc$, tell us how the roots are paired, but they do not yet distinguish the different ways of assigning signs to the two pairs. 
Therefore, we look for a quantity involving the sums of the two pairs.
Choose $T= a+b-c-d$.
This choice is natural because the remaining substitutions act on the two pair sums in a simple way.
For example, $(ab)(cd)$ interchanges $a,b$ and $c,d$ so $a+b-c-d \mapsto b+a-d-c=T$.
Thus this substitution leaves $T$ unchanged. However, $(ac)(bd)$ gives $a+b-c-d \mapsto c+d-a-b=-T$, and similarly for the remaining double swap. Therefore, knowing $T$ will eliminate the substitutions that change its sign.
The difficulty is that $T$ is not directly known. 
To compute it, we compute its square: $T^2 = (a+b-c-d)^2$.
Expanding, $T^2 = a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 + 2ab-2ac-2ad-2bc-2bd+2cd$.
Using $\sigma_1 = a+b+c+d$ and $\sigma_2 = ab+ac+ad+bc+bd+cd$, we have $a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2 = \sigma_1^2 - 2 \sigma_2$.
Substituting and collecting terms gives $T^2 = \sigma_1^2 - 4 \sigma_2 + 4(ab+cd)$.
Since $P= ab+cd$, we obtain $T^2 = 4P+ \sigma_1^2 - 4\sigma_2$.
The right-hand side is already known from the previous step, so $T = \pm \sqrt{\text{known expression}}$.
This introduces the third radical, another square root.
Once $T$ is adjoined to the field already obtained, every remaining substitution must fix $T$.
The substitutions $(ac)(bd)$, $(ad)(bc)$ are no longer possible because the send $T \mapsto -T$.
The only remaining substitutions are therefore $e$, $(ab)(cd)$, and the group is reduced: $G_4 \mapsto G_2$.

Fourth step: $G_2 \to G_1$
After the previous step, the only substitutions that remain possible are $G_2 = \left\{e, (ab)(cd) \right\}$.
The remaining nontrivial substitution is the double interchange $(ab)(cd)$, which swaps $a \leftrightarrow b$, $c \leftrightarrow d$.
To reduce this final group, we need a quantity that is fixed by the identity but changed by this remaining substitution. Since the substitution exchanges the roots within each pair, the natural quantities to consider are differences of roots within a pair.
Choose $W=a-b$.
This choice is natural because the remaining substitution acts on $W$ in the simplest possible way: $(ab)(cd): a-b \mapsto b-a = -(a-b)$.
Thus the two remaining substitutions act as $e: W \mapsto W$, and $(ab)(cd): W \mapsto -W$.
Once $W$ is adjoined to the field generated by the quantities already obtained, the substitution $(ab)(cd)$ is no longer possible, since it does not leave $W$ fixed. 
Hence the group is reduced from $G_2 = \left\{e, (ab)(cd) \right\}$ to $G_1 = \left\{e \right\}$.
The difficulty is that $W$ is not immediately expressible in terms of the quantities already known.
We already know the quantity $T=a+b-c-d$, which involves the sums of the two pairs. To recover the difference $a-b$, we introduce the corresponding combinations involving the differences within the pairs: $T_2 = (a-b)-(c-d)=a-b-c+d$, and $T_3=(a-b)+(c-d)=a-b+c-d$.
These choices are made because their sum gives the quantity we want: $T_2 + T_3 = (a-b-c+d)+(a-b+c-d)=2(a-b)$, so $2W = T_2 + T_3$.
Therefore, it is enough to determine $(T_2 + T_3)^2$.
Expanding, $(T_2+T_3)^2=T_2^2+T_3^2 + 2T_2T_3$.
The squares are already determined from the quantities obtained earlier: $T_2^2 = 4R + \sigma_1^2 - 4 \sigma_2$, and $T_3^2 = 4Q + \sigma_1^2 - 4 \sigma_2$.
Thus the only remaining quantity needed is the product $T_2T_3$.
To obtain this product, consider it together with the already known quantity $T$. A direct calculation gives $TT_2T_3 = \sigma_1^3 - 4\sigma_1\sigma_2 + 8 \sigma_3$.
The right-hand side is expressed entirely in terms of the coefficients of the quartic, so $TT_2T_3$ is known.
Since $T$ is already known, this determines $T_2T_3$.
Hence, $(T_2 + T_3)^2 = T_2^2 + T_3^2 + 2T_2T_3$ is known. 
Because $2W = T_2 + T_3$, we obtain $W^2 = \frac{(T_2 + T_3)^2}{4}$.
Finally, $W = \pm \sqrt{\text{known expression}}$.
This final square root determines $W$ and removes the last remaining substitution: $G_2 \to G_1$.

Fifth step: Recovering the roots
At this point, the necessary quantities have been determined: $\sigma_1=a+b+c+d$, $T=a+b-c-d$, and $W=a-b$.
These give the sums and differences needed to isolate the individual roots.
Adding the expressions for $\sigma_1$ and $T$, $\sigma_1 + T = (a+b+c+d)+(a+b-c-d)=2(a+b)$.
Therefore, $a+b = \frac{\sigma_1+T}{2}$.
Since $W = a-b$, we have $a = \frac{(a+b)+(a-b)}{2}$.
Hence, $a= \frac{\sigma_1 + T + 2W}{4}$.
Similarly, $b=\frac{\sigma_1 + T - 2W}{4}$.
It remains to determine $c$ and $d$. Since $R = ad + bc$, and $Q=ac+bd$, we have $Q-R=ac+bd-ad-bc=(a-b)(c-d)$. 
Therefore, $c-d=\frac{Q-R}{W}$.
Also, $c+d = \sigma_1 - (a+b) = \sigma_1 - \frac{\sigma_1 + T}{2} = \frac{\sigma_1 - T}{2}$.
Hence, $c = \frac{(c+d)+(c-d)}{2}$, and $d=\frac{(c+d)-(c-d)}{2}$.
Thus every root is expressed in terms of quantities already obtained, and the solution of the quartic is complete.

This is the central idea. At each stage, we choose a quantity that is not fixed by the current group of substitutions but transforms in a simple way under it. An appropriate power of that quantity (a square or cube) is invariant, so it can be expressed in terms of the quantities already known and the coefficients of the quartic. Recovering the quantity itself requires adjoining a radical. Thus each reduction of the substitution group corresponds to adjoining one new radical. Then I'd adjust the table slightly:

Notes
- In this context, a quantity simply means some expression built from the roots. It doesn't have to be symmetric. The whole point is to choose expressions whose behavior under permutations is useful.
- Consider $a+b+c+d$. Swap any two roots: $a+b+c+d=b+a+c+d$. Nothing changes. So this quantity gives no information whatsoever about which permutation occurred. It is invariant under all 24 permutations. Knowing its value does not eliminate a single permutation. Now consider $\Delta = (a-b)(a-c)(a-d)(b-c)(b-d)(c-d)$. Suppose we swap $a$ and $b$. Then $(a-b) \to (b-a) = -(a-b)$, while the remaining factors are merely rearranged. Hence $\Delta \to - \Delta$. So this permutation changes the value. But if we perform an even permutation, for example $(a,b,c,d) \mapsto (b,c,a,d)$, then $\Delta$ stays the same. So, after we compute $\Delta$, we immediately know something: if the value is $+\sqrt{\Delta^2}$ only even permutations are possible; if it is $-\sqrt{\Delta^2}$, only odd permutations are possible. Half of the original $24$ permutations have been ruled out.
- "Introducing a quantity" doesn't physically separate the permutations. Rather, it gives you additional information whose value is preserved by only a subgroup of the original permutations. Knowing that value eliminates every permutation that would have changed it. That is why the possible permutation group gets smaller at each step.
