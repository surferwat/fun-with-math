Gauss proved that, for prime integers $p$, the polynomial $X^{p-1} +X^{p-2}+... + X + 1$ is irreducible over the rational field $\mathbb{Q}$. A proof of this fact will be given in $\S64$. Making use of it, prove that the Galois group of $(x^p-1)/(x-1)=0$ is the group Galois described $(\S42)$.

Let $\Phi_p(x) = \frac{x^p-1}{x-1}=x^{p-1} + x^{p-2}+...+x+1$, where $p$ is prime, and let $\zeta$ be a primitive $p$th root of unity. 
By Gauss's theorem $\Phi_p(x)$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$. Hence $\Phi_p(x)$ is the minimal polynomial of $\zeta$, so $[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta):\mathbb{Q}] = \deg \Phi_p = p - 1$.
Since every root of $\Phi_p(x)$ is a power of $\zeta$, namely $\zeta, \zeta^2,..., \zeta^{p-1}$, the splitting field of $\Phi_p(x)$ is $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)$. 
Therefore, $| \operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q}) | = [\mathbb{Q}(\zeta):\mathbb{Q}] = p -1$.

Now let $\sigma \in \operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)/\mathbb{Q})$.
Since automorphisms send roots of the minimal polynomial to roots of the same polynomial, $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^k$ for some $k \in \{1,..., p-1\}$. 
Consequently $\sigma(\zeta^m) = \sigma(\zeta)^m = \zeta^{km}$, so the action of $\sigma$ is completely determined by its value on $\zeta$.
Now choose a primitive root $g \pmod p$, and relabel the roots by $a_0 = \zeta$, $a_1 = \zeta^g$, $a_2 = \zeta^{g^2}$, ..., $a_{p-2} = \zeta^{g^{p-2}}$.
Since $g$ generates the multiplicative group $(\mathbb{Z}/ p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$, these are precisely all the roots of $\Phi_p(x)$.
Suppose $\sigma(a_0) = a_{\mu}$.
Then $\sigma(\zeta) = \zeta^{g^{\mu}}$, and for each $i$, $\sigma(a_i) = \sigma(\zeta^{g^i}) = \sigma(\zeta)^{g^i} = (\zeta^{g^{\mu}})^{g^i} = \zeta^{g^{\mu}g^i} = \zeta^{g^{\mu + i}} = a_{\mu + i}$, where the indices are taken modulo $p-1$ (since $g^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$).
Thus every automorphism acts as one of the cyclic shifts $a_i \mapsto a_{i+\mu}$, which are exactly the substitutions displayed by Galois in $\S42$. 
Hence the Galois group is a subgroup of the cyclic group consisting of these $p-1$ substitutions.
But this group itself has $p-1$ elements, and we have already shown that the Galois group also has $p-1$ elements. Therefore the two groups are equal.
Hence the Galois group of $\frac{x^p-1}{x-1} = 0$ is precisely the group described by Galois in $\S42$.

Notes
- $(\mathbb{Z}/ p\mathbb{Z})^{\times}$ is the set of nonzero residues modulo $p$: $\{ 1, 2, ..., p-1 \}$, where the operation is multiplication, followed by reducing the result modulo $p$.
- A primitive root modulo $p$ is an element $g$ whose powers produce every nonzero residue $\mod p$. For example, if $p=7$, then $g=3$ is a primitive root because $3^1 \equiv 3$, $3^2 \equiv 2$, $3^3 \equiv 6$, $3^4 \equiv 4$, $3^5 \equiv 5$, $3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$. Every nonzero residue appears exactly once. So when we define $a_i = \zeta^{g^i}$, the exponents $g^0, g^1, ..., g^{p-2}$ run through $1,2,..., p-1$ (in some order). Thus $a_0, a_1, ..., a_{p-2}$ are exactly all the primitive $p$th roots of unity.
- $a_{\mu+i}$ means $a_{\mu + i} \pmod {p-1}$.


Excerpt $\S42$ 
Galois gives two examples of Galois groups of equations. The first is what he calls an "algebraic" equation, by which he means an equation in which the coefficients are indeterminates rather than numbers. For such equations, he says that the group is the set of all $n!$ substitutions of the roots "because in this case the symmetric functions [of the roots] are the only one that can be determined rationally." that is, are the only ones in $K$. In other words, only symmetric functions of the roots can be expressed in terms of the coefficients and therefore, by Proposition $I$, the group must contain all substitutions. This is rather plausible, but the proof is far from obvious (See $\S67$).

His other example is the group of the equation $x^{p-1} + x^{p-2} + ... + x + 1 = 0$ satisfied by the primitive $p$th roots of unity where $p$ is prime. Here if $a$ is any root of the equation the other roots are $a^2,a^3,...,a^{p-1}$. If $S$ is any element of the Galois group, then $S(a) =a^k$ for some $k=1,2,...,p-1$ (because $S(a)$ is a root). Thus the effect of $S$ on any root is known because $S(a^j) = S(a)^j=a^{jk}$ ($S$ is an automorphism). The substitutions $S(a^j)=a^k$ are more conveniently described by choosing a primitive root $g \mod p$ (see $\S24$) and setting $a_0=a, a_1 = a_0^g, a_2=a_1^g, a_3 = a_2^g,...$ Then $S$ carries $a_i$ to $a_{i+\mu}$, where $\mu$ is defined by $S(a_0)=a_{\mu}$, because $S(a_1)=S(a_0^g)=S(a_0)^g = a_{\mu}^g = a_{\mu+1}, S(a_2)=a_{\mu+2},...$ Thus the group of this equation is contained in the group presented by:
a_0 a_1 a_2 ... a_{p-2}
a_1 a_2 a_3 ... a_0
a_2 a_3 a_4 ... a_1
. . . ... .
a_{p-2} a_0 a_1 ... a_{p-3}

The group depends on the field $K$ of known quantities. Galois, clearly considering the case where $K$ is the field of rational numbers, states, without proof, that the group is the set of all $p-1$ substitutions presented in the table above. The proof of this fact uses a theorem of Gauss (Exercise 10).

Another example of interest is an equation of the form $x^p =k$ where the known quantities $K$ include $k$ and a primitive $p$th root of unity $\alpha$(with $p$ prime). If $a$ is any root of the equation $x^p - k =0$ then the other roots are $\alpha a$, $\alpha^2 a$, ..., $\alpha^{p-1}a$. If $S$ is any substitution of the Galois group then $S(a) = \alpha^j a$ for some $j$ and all values of $S$ are given by $S(\alpha^k a) = \alpha^kS(a)=\alpha^{k+j}a$ (because $S$ is an automorphism that leaves the elements of $K$ fixed). Thus,

$a$ $\alpha a$ $\alpha^2 a$ ... $\alpha^{p-1}a$
$\alpha a$ $\alpha^2 a$ $\alpha^3a$ ... $a$
$\alpha^2 a$ $\alpha^3 a$  $\alpha^4 a$ ... $\alpha a$
. . . ... .
$\alpha^{p-1}a$ $a$ $\alpha a$ ... $\alpha^{p-2} a$

is a presentation of a group containing the Galois group. Since this is a group with $p$ elements, and since the number of elements in a subgroup must therefore divide $p$, the Galois group is either the entire group presented above or it is the group containing the identity alone. In the later case, by Proposition I, every element of $K(a,b,c,...)$ is in $K$ and the equation already has $p$ roots in $K$. Otherwise the Galois group is the group presented above. A simple consequence of this observation is that if $k$ does not have a $p$th root in $K$ and if $K$ contains a primitive $p$th root of unity then the polynomial $x^p - k$ is irreducible over $K$ (see Exercise 4).

As a final example, consider the equation $x^4 + 1 = 0$ (the equation for the primitive 8th roots of unity). If $a$ is any root then so are $a^3$, $a^5$, and $a^7$, and the group of the equation is easily seen to be contained in the group presented by 

a a^3 a^5 a^7
a^3 a a^7 a^5
a^5 a^7 a a^3
a^7 a^5 a^3 a

As before, if $K$ is the field of rational numbers, then the Galois group of the equation is the entire group of four elements presented above (Exercise 11).

For explicit presentations of the Galois groups of cubic equations, see Exercise 6 and 7.
