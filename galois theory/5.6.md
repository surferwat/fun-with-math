Let $x^3+px+q$ be an irreducible cubic whose discriminant is a square, say $P^2 = - 4p^3-27q^2$. Let $K(t)$ be obtained by adjoining a root $t$ of $t^3 + 3pt + P = 0$. Give explicit second degree polynomials $a,b,c$ in $t$ with coefficients in $K$ which are roots of $x^3+px+q=0$ in $K(t)$. Show that $t' = b - c$ and $t'' = c-a$ are conjugates of $t$ and find the Galois group.

Let $f(x) = x^3 + px + q$ be irreducible over $K$, with roots $a,b,c$.
Let $g(X) = X^3 + 3pX + P \in K[X]$ where $P^2 = -4p^3 -27q^2 = P^2$ is the discriminant of $f$.
By Viete's formulas applied to a monic cubic, $a+b+c = 0$, $ab+bc+ca=p$, and $abc=-q$.
Assume the discriminant $\Delta = -4p^3 -27q^2 = P^2$ is a square in $K$.
Let $t=a-b$.
Then: $K(a,b,c) = K(t)$, $t$ satisfies $g(t)=t^3 + 3pt + P = 0$.
Since $t \notin K$ and $g$ has degree $3$, the minimal polynomial of $t$ over $K$ has degree $3$, hence $[K(t) : K] = 3$, so $\{1,t,t^2\}$ is a basis of $K(t)$.

**Step 1: Express $a,b$ in terms of $c,t$**
Let $t=a-b$.
Since $a+b+c = 0$, we have $a=\frac{t-c}{2}$, $b=\frac{-t-c}{2}$.
So it suffices to determine $c \in K(t)$.

**Step 2: Derive rational expression for $c$**
From $ab+bc+ca =p$, substituting the expressions for $a,b$, we obtain: $3c^2+t^2=-4p$.
Hence, $c^2 = - \frac{t^2+4p}{3}$.
From $abc = -q$, using $ab=\frac{c^2-t^2}{4}$, we get: $c(c^2-t^2)=-4q$.
Substituting $c^2$ and simplifying gives: $c = \frac{3q}{t^2+p}$.

**Step 3: Derive polynomial expression for $c$**
By assumption, $t^3+3pt+P=0 \Rightarrow t^3=-3pt-P$.
Then, multiplying by $t$, we have $t^4 = -3pt^2 -Pt$.
Now use Exercise 8, which showed that $t$ satisfies $(t^2+4p)(t^2+p)^2 + 27q^2 = 0$.
Rearranging, $(t^2+p)^2(t^2+4p) = -27q^2$.
Since $q \ne 0$, $\frac{1}{t^2+p} = - \frac{(t^2+p)(t^2+4p)}{27q^2}$
Substituting into $c = \frac{3q}{t^2+p}$, we get $c=3q \cdot \frac{1}{t^2+p} =-\frac{(t^2+p)(t^2+4p)}{9q} = -\frac{t^4+5pt^2+4p^2}{9q}$.
Now substituting $t^4 = -3pt^2 - Pt$, we get $c = \frac{Pt -2pt^2-4p^2}{9q}$.

**Step 4: Derive polynomial expressions for $a$ and $b$**
Substituting in $c$ into $a$, we get $a = \frac{t-c}{2} = \frac{2pt^2+(18q-P)t+4p^2}{18q}$.
Substituting in $c$ into $b$, we get $b= \frac{-t-c}{2} = \frac{2pt^2-(18q+P)t+4p^2}{18q}$.
Thus, we have all roots as quadratic polynomials in $t \in K(t)$ as we asked of us in the problem.

**Step 5: Show conjugates of $t$**
From $t= a-b$, we apply permutations to the roots $a,b,c$: swap $a \to b \to c \to a$, then define: $t'=b-c$ and $t''=c-a$.
$t',t''$ satisfy $X^3+3pX+P=0$, so $t',t''$ are roots of the same polynomial as $t$.
Since $t$ is a root of an irreducible cubic over $K$, its minimal polynomial is $X^3+3px+P$. 
Thus, $t,t',t''$ are roots of a minimal polynomial $\Rightarrow$ $t,t',t''$ are conjugates of one another.
Thus $t',t''$ are conjugates of $t$ as was to be shown.

**Step 6: Find the Galois group**
From Exercise 5.5, the the discriminant being a square implies that no odd permutation lies in the Galois group.
Hence the Galois group is contained in the alternating group: $G \subseteq A_3$.
	Since the $f(x)$ is irreducible, its Galois group acts transitively on the roots $a,b,c$. 
Therefore $G$ must contain a $3-cycle$.
The only subgroup of $S_3$ that is both: contained in $A_3$ and transitive on $\{a,b,c\}$, is $A_3 = \{1, (abc),(acb)\}$.
Hence $Gal(f/K) = A_3$.
So the Galois group is $\{(a,b,c), (b,c,a), (c,a,b)\}$.

$\S41$ The Galois group of the given equation $f(x) = 0$ with roots $a,b,c,...$ is the group with the following presentation. Let each of the roots $a,b,c,...$ be expressed in terms of the Galois resolvent $t$, say $a=\phi_a(t), b=\phi_b(t), c=\phi_c(t), ...$ where the $\phi$'s are polynomials with coefficients in $K$. As above, let $F(X)$ be the polynomial of degree $n!$ whose roots are the $n!$ distinct elements $ASa+BSb+CSc+...$ of $K(a,b,c,...)$ where $t = Aa + Bb + Cc + ...$ and where $S$ is one of the $n!$ substitutions of $a,b,c,...$ Finally, let $G(X)$ be an irreducible (over $K$) factor of $F(X)$ of which $t$ is a root. (See $\S38$.) The conjugates $t',t'',$ of $t ($over $K)$ are the roots of $G(X)$, The Galois group of $f(x)=0$ is presented by

$\phi_a(t) \phi_b(t) \phi_c(t) ...$,
$\phi_a(t') \phi_b(t') \phi_c(t') ...$,
$\phi_a(t'') \phi_b(t'') \phi_c(t'') ...$,
$...$

The number of rows in the table - and therefore, the number of elements in the group - is equal to the number of roots $t$, $t'$, $t''$ ... of $G$, which is equal to $deg \, G$. (Note that the conjugates $t'$, $t''$, ... are all roots of $F$ and are therefore of the form $ASa + BSb + CSc + ...$ where $S$ is a substitution of $a,b,c,...$)

In order to justify this definition of the Galois group it must be shown that: 
(A) for any conjugate $t'$ of $t$, the elements $\phi_a(t'), \phi_b(t'), \phi_c(t'),...$ are an arrangement of the 1st row $\phi_a(t) = a$, $\phi_b(t)=b$, $\phi_c(t)=c,...$, 
(B) the arrangements in the table present a group; and
(C) the group of substitutions of the roots $a,b,c,...$ presented by the table is independent of the choice of the Galois resolvent $t$.

In his proof of $(A)$ and in several later proofs, Galois makes use of a simple and very basic lemma:

Lemma I. If $g(X)$ and $h(X)$ are polynomials with coefficients in a given field $K$. if $g(X)$ is irreducible, and if $g(X)$ and $h(X)$ have a common root, then $g(X)$ divides $h(X)$.

Proof. Let the Euclidean algorithm be used to write a common divisor $d(X)$ of $g(X)$ and $h(X)$ in the form $d(X) = A(X)g(X) + B(X)h(X)$ where $A(X)$ and $B(X)$ are polynomials with coefficients in $K$. If the common root $r$ of $g(X)$ and $h(X)$ is substituted into this equation the result is $d(r) = 0$. Therefore $d(X)$ has degree > 0. Since $d(X)$ divides $g(X)$ and $g(X)$ is irreducible, it follows that $d(X)$ is a nonzero element of $K$ times $g(X)$. Since $d(X)$ divides $h(X)$, it follows that $g(X)$ divides $h(X)$, as was to be shown.

Using this lemma, Galois proves $(A)$ as follows. Since $f(\phi_a(X))$ is a polynomial in $X$ with coefficients in $K$, and since it has the root $t$ in common with the irreducible polynomial $G(X)$, by Lemma I it is divisble by $G(X)$ and every root of $G(X)$ is a root of $f(\phi_a(X))$. Similarly, every root of $G(X)$ is a root of $f(\phi_b(X)), f(\phi_c(X)),...$ This shows that the entries in the presentation $(1)$ of the Galois group are all roots of the equation $f(X)=0$. If two entries in the same row are equal, there is an equation of the form $\phi_d(t')=\phi_e(t')$, where $d$ and $e$ are roots of $f(x)=0$ and $t'$ is a root of $G(X)$. By lemma I, $G(X)$ divides $\phi_d(X) - \phi_e(X)$ and therefore $t$ is a root of $\phi_d(X) - \phi_e(X)$. Thus $d=\phi_d(t)=\phi_e(t)=e$. Thus, each row of the presentation of the Galois group includes each root of $f(x)=0$ at most once, and $(A)$ follows.

Galois does not address $(B)$ and $(C)$ directly, but it is clear that he saw them as consequences of his Proposition I. His statement of this Proposition requires rather a lot of explanation about the "substitutions" it is talking about and the type of "invariance" it means. Therefore a reformulation of it seems preferable to a quotation. I believe that this reformulation is faithful to his meaning.

Proposition I. Let $\Psi(U, V, W, ...)$ be a polynomial in $n$ variables with co-efficients in $K$. Let $\Psi_{t'}$ be the element of $K(a,b,c,...)$ obtained by setting $U = \phi_a(t'), V = \phi_b(t'), W = \phi_c(t'), ...$ in $\Psi$. In other words, for $U,V,W,...$ one substitutes the arrangement of the roots given in the row of the above table $(1)$ corresponding to $t'$. Similarly, let $\Psi_t, \Psi_{t''}, \Psi_{t'''}, ...$ be defined by substituting the corresponding row. Then $\Psi_t$ is in $K$ if and only if the elements $\Psi_t, \Psi_{t'}, \Psi_{t''}, ...$ are all equal. Loosely speaking, $\Psi(a,b,c,...)$ is a known quantity if and only if it is invariant under all the substitutions of the Galois group.

Proof. Substitute $U = \phi_a(X), V = \phi_b(X), W = \phi_c(X), ...$ in $\Psi(U,V,W,...)$ to obtain a polynomial $\Psi^*(X)$ with coefficients in $K$. If $\Psi$, is in $K$ then $\Psi^*(X) - \Psi_t$ is a polynomial with coefficients in $K$ of which $t$ is a root. By Lemma I, $G(X)$ divides $\Psi^*(X) - \Psi_t$, and therefore $t'$ is a root of this polynomial when $t'$ is any root of $G$. Thus $\Psi_{t'}-\Psi_t = 0$ and, since $t'$ was arbitrary, $\Psi_t'$, $\Psi_{t''}$, ... are all equal to $\Psi_t$. Conversely, if $\Psi_t$, $\Psi_{t'}$, $\Psi_{t''}$, ... are all equal and if there are $k$ of them then $\Psi_t = \frac{1}{k}[\Psi_t + \Psi_{t'} + \Psi_{t''} + ...] = \frac{1}{k}[\Psi^*(t) + \Psi^*(t') + \Psi^*(t'') + ...]$. This is a symmetric polynomial in the roots $t,t',t'',...$ of $G(X)$ and can therefore be expressed in terms of the coefficients of $G(X)$. Since these coefficients are in $K$, it follows that $\Psi_t$ is in $K$ as was to be shown. 

In order to deduce $(B)$ and $(C)$ from Proposition I, it is helpful to observe:

Corollary. Let $t$ be a Galois resolvent, let $t'$ be one of its conjugates, and let $S$ be the substitution of the roots which carries the row of the above table $(1)$ corresponding to $t$ to the row corresponding to $t'$. In short, let $S(a) = \phi_a(t'), S(b) = \phi_b(t'), S(c)=\phi_c(t'), ...$ Then $S$ can be extended to an automorphism of the entire field $K(a,b,c,...)$ over $K$, that is a function $S$ from $K(a,b,c,...)$ to itself which is the identity map on $K$, which carries sums to sums, products to products, and agrees with the original $S$ for the roots $a,b,c,...$

Proof. An element of $K(a,b,c,...)$ can be written as a polynomial in $t$. Since $t$ can be assumed to be a linear polynomial in $a,b,c,...$ an element of $K(a,b,c, ...)$ can therefore be written as a polynomial in $a,b,c,...$ In other words, any given element of $K(a,b,c,...)$ can be written in the form $\Psi(a,b,c,...)$ where $\Psi$ is a polynomial in $n$ variables with coefficients in $K$. If there is an automorphism extending $S$, it clearly must carry this element to $\Psi(Sa, Sb, Sc,...)$. One could define the effect of the automorphism on the given element to be $\Psi(Sa, Sb, Sc,...)$ if it were know that this definition was independent of the choice of the representation $\Psi(a,b,c,...)$ of the given element. But this follows immediately from Proposition I because if $\Psi(a,b,c,...) = \Phi(a,b,c,...)$ then their difference is $0$ which lies in $K$, and by Proposition I the substitution $S$ of the roots leaves their difference unchanged, that is $\Psi(Sa,Sb,Sc,...) = \Phi(Sa,Sb,Sc,...)$ as was to be shown.

Corollary. Let $t$ be a Galois resolvent, let $t'$ be one of its conjugates, and let $S$ be the corresponding substitution of the roots as in the previous corollary. Let $s$ be any other Galois resolvent of the same equation. Then there is a conjugate $s'$ of $s$ such that $S$ is equal to the substitution of the roots corresponding to $s$ and $s'$.

Proof. Since $s$ is in $K(a,b,c,...)$, the extension of $S$ defined in the preceding corollary applies to $s$. Define $s'$ to be $S(s)$. If $H(x)$ is an irreducible polynomial of which $s$ is a root, then application of $S$ to $H(s) = 0$ gives $H(s') = 0$, so that $s'$ is a conjugate of $s$. Now if $a=\Psi_a(s)$, where $\Psi_a$ is a polynomial with coefficients in $K$, then application of $S$ to both sides gives $S(a) = \Psi_a(Ss) = \Psi_a(s')$. Similarly, $S(b) = \Psi_b(s'), S(c) = \Psi_c(s'),...$, as was to be shown. 

Now $(B)$ follows immediately from the last corollary because this corollary shows that if $t'$ is any conjugate of $t$ then (set $s = t'$) the substitutions of the roots that result from changing $t$ to one of its conjugates are the same as those that result from changing $t'$ to one of its conjugates. (The conjugates of $t$ coincide with those of $t'$ because in both cases the conjugates are the roots of $G$.) Since $(C)$ follows even more immediately from the last corollary, this completes the justification of the above definition of the Galois group of the equation $f(x) = 0$ as a group of substitutions of the roots of the equation. Moreover, it shows that the elements of the Galois group can also be regarded as automorphisms of the field $K(a,b,c,...)$ over $K$ - a fact which is not stated explicitly by Galois, but which is fundamental in most modern formulations of his theory.
