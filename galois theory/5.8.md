Let $f(x)$ be an irreducible cubic whose Galois group has three elements. Then every element $K(t)$ can be written as a polynomial of degree $<3$ in $t$ with coefficients in $K$. In particular, $1,a,$ and $a^2$ can be so expressed, where $a$ is any root of $f$. Show, without computation, that these equations can be solved for $t$ as a polynomial in $a$. Thus the other roots of $f$ can be expressed as polynomials in $a$. Find these expressions, assuming $\sigma_1 = 0$. Apply them in the cases $f(x) = x^3 -3x+1$ and $f(x) = x^3 -2$ to express the remaining two roots in terms of a given one.

Let $f(x)$ be an irreducible cubic over $K$ whose Galois group has order $3$, and let $a$ be one of its roots. 
Let $t$ be a Galois resolvent.
Since the Galois group has three elements, $[K(t) : K] = 3$. Hence every element of $K(t)$ can be written uniquely as a polynomial of degree $< 3$ in $t$. 
In particular, $1,a,a^2$ can each be expressed in the basis $\{1,t,t^2\}$. 
Thus, there are coefficients in $K$ s.t. $1 =  \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 t + \alpha_2 t^2$, $a = \beta_0 + \beta_1 t + \beta_2 t^2$, $a^2 = \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 t + \gamma_2 t^2$. 

We wish to solve these equations for $1,t,t^2$. 
Suppose this were impossible. 
If the equations could not be solved for $1,t,t^2$ then the three equations would not be independent. Hence there would exist constants $\lambda, \mu, \nu \in K$, not all zero, satisfying $\lambda \cdot 1 + \mu a + \nu a^2 = 0$.
But this says that $a$ satisfies a polynomial over $K$ of degree at most $2$, contradicting the assumptions that $a$ has irreducible cubic minimal polynomial since a number cannot have both a quadratic and a cubic minimal polynomial.
Hence, the system is solvable. In particular $t = \mu_0 + \mu_1 a + \mu_2 a^2$ for suitable $\mu_0, \mu_1, \mu_2 \in K$.
Now every root of $f$ is already known to be a polynomial in $t$. 
Substituting the above expression for $t$, every root is therefore a polynomial of degree at most $2$ in $a$. 
Thus, the remaining roots $b$ and $c$ can be written as quadratic polynomials in $a$.

Assume now that $f(x) = x^3 + px + q, a+b+c = 0$.
Let $P^2 = -4p^3-27q^2$, the discriminant of $f$.
Since the Galois group has order $3$, the discriminant is a square in $K$, so $P \in K$.
Solving for the coefficients of the quadratic expressions for $b$ and $c$ (using the relations among the roots) gives $b,c = -\frac{a}{2} \pm \frac{6pa^2-9qa+4p^2}{2P}$. 
The two choices of sign simply interchange $b$ and $c$.

**Example 1**
Let $f(x) = x^3 -3x +1$.
Then $p=-3, q=1$, and $P^2 = -4(-3)^3 - 27(1)^2 = 81$, so we may take $P=9$.
Therefore $b,c = - \frac{a}{2} \pm \frac{-18a^2-9a+36}{18} = -\frac{a}{2}\pm \frac{-2a^2-a+4}{2}$.
Choosing one sign gives $b=a^2-2$, while the other gives $c=-a^2-a+2$.

**Example 2**
Let $f(x) = x^3 -2$.
Here $p=0, q=-2$, and $P^2 = -108$, so assuming $\sqrt{-3} \in K$, $P = 6 \sqrt{-3}$.
Then $b,c = -\frac{a}{2} \pm \frac{18a}{12 \sqrt{-3}} = - \frac{a}{2} \pm \frac{3a}{2\sqrt{-3}}$.
Since $\frac{1}{\sqrt{-3}} = - \frac{\sqrt{-3}}{3}$, this simplifies to $b,c = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{-3}}{2}a$.
These are exactly the two non-real cube roots of $2$: $\omega a$, $\omega^2 a$, where $\omega = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2}$.

Notes
- Harold's solution: If the expressions of $1,a,a^2$ in terms of $1,t,t^2$ can not be solved for $1,t,t^2$ in terms of $1,a,a^2$ then $1,a,a^2$ satisfy a linear relation $\alpha \cdot 1 + \beta \cdot a + \gamma \cdot a^2 = 0$ (with $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ in $K$) contrary to the assumption that $a$ is the root of an irreducible cubic, $b,c = - (a/2) \pm (6pa^2-9qa + 4p^2/2P)$. In the first case $b,c = a^2-2$, $-a^2-a+2$. In the second, $b,c = (-1 \pm \sqrt{-3})a/2$. In the second case it is assumed, of course, that $\sqrt{-3} \in K$ so that the discriminant is a square.
- A key step is showing that from $1,a,a^2 \in K(t)$ you can recover: $1,t,t^2 \in K(a)$. This is conceptually important because it says that the two "coordinate systems" $(1,a,a^2)$ and $(1,t,t^2)$ are just two bases of the same 3-dimensional vector space over $K$, so the change-of-basis matrix is invertible. The argument correctly uses a contradiction via linear dependence, which is exactly the right idea: if inversion failed, you would force a nontrivial relation among $1,a,a^2$ contradicting irreducibility.
