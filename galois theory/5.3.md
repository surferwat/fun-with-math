Show that a subgroup $H$ of a group $G$ is normal in the sense defined in $\S40$ if and only if for every $S$ in $H$ and $T$ in $G$, $T^{-1}ST$ is in $H$.

Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$. 
In the notation of $\S40$, the various subpresentations of $H$ are obtained by multiplying every element of $H$ on the left by a fixed element of $G$. 
Thus a typical subpresentation has first row $T, TS_1, TS_2, ...$, where $S_1, S_2, ... \in H$.
Each row is of the form $TS$ with $S \in H$, so the same computation applies uniformly to all rows.
We shall prove that the condition of $\S40$ is equivalent to $T^{-1}ST \in H$ $(\forall S \in H, \forall T \in G)$

Suppose first that $H$ is normal in the sense of $\S40$.
Let $S \in H$ and $T, U \in G$. 
Consider the subpresentations whose first rows begin with $T$ and $U$, respectively. 
The substitution carrying the first element $T$ to $U$ is $UT^{-1}$.

Since $H$ is normal in the sense of $\S40$, this substitution carries the whole first row of the $T-$subpresentation to the corresponding row of the $U-$subpresentation.

Now $TS$ is an entry in the first row of the $T-$subpresentation. 
Applying $UT^{-1}$ replaces the base element $T$ by $U$, and correspondingly transforms $S$ by conjugation $T^{-1}ST$, so  $UT^{-1}(TS) = U(T^{-1}ST)$.
Since the image must lie in the corresponding row of the $U-$subpresentation, it must be an entry of that row.
Every entry of the $U-$subpresentation has the form $UK$ with $K \in H$.
Hence $U(T^{-1}ST) = UK$ for some $K \in H$. 
Cancelling the initial $U$, we obtain $T^{-1}ST = K \in H$. 
Since $S$ and $T$ were arbitrary, $T^{-1}ST \in H$ $(\forall S \in H,$ $\forall T \in G)$.

Conversely, suppose that 
$T^{-1}ST \in H$  $(\forall S \in H, \forall T \in G)$.
Let $UT^{-1}$ be a substitution carrying the first row of one subpresentation to the first row of another.
Take any entry $TS$ in the first row of the first subpresentation, where $S \in H$. Then $UT^{-1}(TS) = U(T^{-1}ST)$.
By hypothesis, $T^{-1}ST \in H$. Therefore $U(T^{-1}ST)$ is an entry of the corresponding row of the second subpresentation.
Thus every entry of the first row is carried into the corresponding entry of the second row. The same calculation applies to any entry in any corresponding row of the two subpresentations, since each row is obtained from the subgroup elements by left multiplication with a fixed group element. 
Therefore a substitution carrying the first row of one subpresentation to the first row of another also carries the following rows correspondingly.
Hence $H$ is normal in the sense of $\S40$.
Therefore a subgroup $H$ is normal in the sense of $\S40$ if and only if $T^{-1}ST \in H$  $(\forall S \in H, \forall T \in G)$.

Notes
- This problem is doing something more important than it first looks like: it is repackaging normal subgroups into Galois' original language of "presentations of arrangements". The statement $T^{-1}ST \in H$ $(\forall S \in H, \forall T \in G)$ is the modern definition of a normal subgroup. So the problem is proving: "Galois' geometric/symmetric notion of 'normality of presentations' is exactly conjugation-invariance". It shows that Galois' idea of "equivalent presentations of a subgroup is exactly the modern algebraic notion of a normal subgroup defined by conjugation invariance".
- $UT^{-1}(TS)$ here means first apply $T^{-1}$, then apply $U$ to the arrangement $TS$.
- We write $TS$ instead of $ST$ because in Edwards' convention substitutions act on arrangements from the right, so the subgroup element modifies the base arrangement on its right.
- $T^{-1}(TS) = (T^{-1}T)S$ is not a valid simplification in Edwards' notation. If we were to do this we would be switching between two interpretations: algebraic multiplication of symbols and action of substitutions on arrangements (Edwards' meaning). We must treat $TS$ as a structured object, "apply $S$ inside the $T$-row". Then the rule is $UT^{-1}(TS) = U(T^{-1}ST)$ and this is not derived by cancelling $T^{-1}T$.

An arrangement of the $n$ roots $a,b,c$, ... is simply a listing of them in some order. A substitution of them is a one-to-one onto mapping of the roots to themselves. Two arrangements can be regarded as representing a substitution, namely, the substitution which transforms the first arrangement into the second. For example, the ordered pair (abcdef, bacefd) represents the substitution $d->e->f->d$ of $d,e$ and $f$. A presentation of a group is a list of arrangements of $a,b,c$, ... with the property that the set of substitutions which transforms the first arrangement in the list to each of the others is the same as the set of substitutions which carry any other arrangement in the list to the remaining ones. For example below is a presentation of a group:

abcdef
bacefd
abcdfde
bacdef
abcefd
bacfde

If the columns are rearranged so that the 1st row is in the order of the 4th row, say, the table becomes

bacdef
abcefd
bacfde
abcdef
bacefd
abcfde

One the one hand, rearranging the columns does not alter the substitutions; therefore, the two tables describe the same substitutions. On the other hand, the rows of the second table are a rearrangement of those of the first table to the remaining rows. In order to show that the first table (and therefore the second table) is a presentation of a group, one would have to show that what was just shown to be true of the 4th row is true of the other rows also.

A group of substitutions is a set of substitutions that can be obtained in this way from a presentation of a group, including the identity substitution which carries the first arrangement to itself. Galois observes that a set of substitutions is a group if and only if it is closed under composition, that is, if and only if for any two substitutions $S$ and $T$ in the set, their composition $ST$ is in the set. In the example above, let $S$ be the substitution which carries the 1st row to the 4th ($S$ is the interchange $a \leftrightarrow b$) and let $T$ be the one which carries the $1$st row to the 5th ($T$ is the cyclic substitution $d \to e \to f \to d$) Then $S$ and $T$ commute, $S^2=T^3=$ identity, and the six rows of the table correspond to the identity substitution, $ST, T^2, S, T,$ and $ST^2$. Since this set of substitutions is closed under composition, it follows that the table is a presentation of a group.
