Show that the solution of an $n$th degree equation of the form $x^n+a x^{n-1}+bx^{n-2}+...+dx+\epsilon=0$ can be reduced to the solution of another such equation in which $a=0$ by the substitution $x=y+C$.

$\mathit{Proof}$
WTS: by substituting $x=y+C$, the equation can be rewritten in the same form, but with the $y^{n−1}$ term having coefficient zero.
Substituting $x=y+C$, we get: $(y+C)^n+a (y+C)^{n-1}+...+d(y+C)+\epsilon=0$.
We focus on $(y+C)^n$  and $(y+C)^{n-1}$ since higher order terms only contribute to powers lower than $y^{n-1}$ and our goal is to eliminate the $y^{n-1}$ term.
We expand $(y+C)^n$ and $(y+C)^{n-1}$ using the binomial theorem:
$(y+C)^n=\binom{n}{0} y^n+\binom{n}{1} y^{n-1}C+...+\binom{n}{n} y^0C^n=y^n+ny^{n-1}C+...+C^n$
$(y+C)^{n-1}=\binom{n-1}{0} y^{n-1}+\binom{n-1}{1} y^{n-2}C+...+\binom{n-1}{n-1} y^0C^{n-1}= y^{n-1}+(n-1) y^{n-2}C+...+C^{n-1}$
Substituting $(y+C)^n$ and $(y+C)^{n-1}$, we get:
$(y^n+ny^{n-1}C+...+C^n)+a(y^{n-1}+(n-1)y^{n-2}C+...+C^{n-1})+d(y+C)+\epsilon$
$=(y^n+ny^{n-1}C+...+C^n)+(ay^{n-1}+a(n-1)y^{n-2}C+...+aC^{n-1})+dy+dC+\epsilon$
$=(y^n+ny^{n-1}C+ay^{n-1}+a(n-1)y^{n-2}C+...+aC^{n-1}+C^n)+dy+dC+\epsilon$
$=(y^n+(nC+a)y^{n-1}+a(n-1)y^{n-2}C+...+aC^{n-1}+C^n)+dy+dC+\epsilon$.
We re-express this formula changing variables to $\tilde{b}$, $\tilde{d}$, and $\tilde{\epsilon}$ for clarity:
$y^n+(nC+a) y^{n-1}+\tilde{b}y^{n-2}+...+\tilde{d}y+\tilde{\epsilon}=0$.
Setting the coefficient for $y^{n-1}$ to $0$ and solving for $C$, we get $nC+a=0 \implies C=-\frac{a}{n}$.
Substituting $C$ for $-\frac{a}{n}$, we get:
$y^n+(n(-\frac{a}{n})+a) y^{n-1}+\tilde{b}y^{n-2}+...+\tilde{d}y+\tilde{\epsilon}=0$.
$\implies y^n+(0) y^{n-1}+\tilde{b}y^{n-2}+...+\tilde{d}y+\tilde{\epsilon}=0$.
$\implies y^n+\tilde{b}y^{n-2}+...+\tilde{d}y+\tilde{\epsilon}=0$.
Thus, substituting in $x=y-\frac{a}{n}$, we reduced the $n$th degree equation to another such equation in which the $y^{n-1}$ coefficient is equal to $0$. $\blacksquare$



Notes
- This exercise demonstrates how polynomial equations of the form $x^n+a x^{n-1}+bx^{n-2}+...+dx+\epsilon=0$ can be transformed into a similar form without the $x^{n-1}$ term: $y^n +$ (lower order terms) $= 0$, reducing complexity when trying to solve or analyze the equation, either algebraically or numerically.
- The idea originates from Tschirnhaus transformations.

Special case
2nd degree equation $x^2+a x+\epsilon=0$.
Let $x=y+C$.
Substituting $x$ for $y+C$, we get $(y+C)^2+a(y+C)+\epsilon=0$
$\implies y^2+2yC+C^2+ay+aC+\epsilon=0$.
$\implies y^2+(2C+a)y+(C^2+aC+\epsilon)=0$.
Setting the coefficient for $y^{n-1}$ to $0$ and solving for $C$, we get $2C+a=0 \implies C=-\frac{a}{2}$.
Substituting $C$ for $-\frac{a}{2}$, we get: $y^2+(2(-\frac{a}{2})+a)y+((-\frac{a}{2})^2+a(-\frac{a}{2})+\epsilon)=0$
$\implies y^2+\frac{a^2}{4}-\frac{2a^2}{4}+\epsilon=0$
$\implies y^2-\frac{a^2}{4}+\epsilon=0$.
Thus, substituting in $x=y-\frac{a}{2}$ we have reduced the $2$nd degree equation to another such equation in which $y^{n-1}$ coefficient is equal to $0$.
Although we are not expected to show that a solution to $y^2-\frac{a^2}{4}+\epsilon=0$ is a solution to $x^2+a x+\epsilon=0$ since it is implied by substitution, we can do so as a check.
Rearranging terms and solving for $y$, we get $y^2=\frac{a^2}{4}-\epsilon \implies y=\pm\sqrt{\frac{a^2}{4}-\epsilon}$.
Thus, $x=y+C=\pm\sqrt{\frac{a^2}{4}-\epsilon}+-\frac{a}{2}$.
Rearranging terms we see that this is equivalent to the quadratic formula: 
$x=-\frac{a}{2}\pm\frac{\sqrt{a^2-4\epsilon}}{2}=\frac{-a\pm\sqrt{a^2-4\epsilon}}{2}$.
