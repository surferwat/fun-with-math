Give a proof of the fundamental theorem on symmetric polynomials along the following lines. Let $G(x_1,x_2,..., x_n)$ be a symmetric polynomial, and let $Ax_1^{m_1}x_2^{m_2}...x_n^{m_n}$ be its leading term (in lexicographic order). Then $m_1 \ge m_2 \ge ... \ge m_n$ and $A\sigma_1^{m_1-m_2}\sigma_2^{m_2-m_3} ... \sigma_{n-1}^{m_{n-1}-m_n}\sigma_n^{m_n} = f$ is a symmetric polynomial with the same leading term. Similarly, let $g$ be the monomial in $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, ..., \sigma_n$ with the same leading term as $G-f$, $h$ the monomial with the same leading term as $G-f-g$, and so forth. Show that eventually one of these differences must be $0$, and therefore that $G$ can be written as a sum of monomials in $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, ..., \sigma_n$ with coefficients that are integers if the coefficients of $G$ are integers.

$G(x_1,x_2,..., x_n) = Ax_1^{m_1}x_2^{m_2}...x_n^{m_n} + ...$
Indeed, if any $m_i <m_{i+1}$​, then permuting $x_i \leftrightarrow x_{i+1}$ could produce a monomial larger in lexicographic order, contradicting that our term was the leading one in a symmetric polynomial.

$f = A\sigma_1^{m_1-m_2}\sigma_2^{m_2-m_3} ... \sigma_{n-1}^{m_{n-1}-m_n}\sigma_n^{m_n}$
$\sigma_1 = x_1 + ...$, $\sigma_2 = x_1x_2 + ...$, $\sigma = x_1x_2x_2 + ...$
Therefore, the leading term of $f$ is $A(x_1)^{m_1-m_2}(x_1x_2)^{m_1-m_2} ... (x_1x_2...x_n)^{m_n} = Ax_1^{m_1}x_2^{m_2}x_n^{m_n}$
Thus, $f$ is symmetric and has the same leading term as $G$.

Define $G_1 = G - f$.
$G_1$ is still symmetric but its leading term is (in lexicographical order) is strictly smaller than the leading term of $G$.
Take the leading term of $G_1$ say $A'x_1^{m_1'}...x_n^{m_n'}$.
Define $f_1 = A'\sigma_1^{m_1'-m_2'}\sigma_1\sigma_2^{m_1'-m_2'} ... \sigma_1\sigma_2...\sigma_n^{m_n'}$.
Therefore, the leading term of $G_1$ is $A'x_1^{m_1'-m_2'}x_1x_2^{m_1'-m_2'} ... x_1x_2...x_n^{m_n'} = A'x_1^{m_1'}x_2^{m_2'}x_n^{m_n'}$.

Then $G_2 = G_1 - f_1$ which again has a smaller leading term.
Because there are only finitely many monomials in any polynomial $G$, this process must terminate after finitely many steps.
At each step, the leading term strictly decreases in lexicographical order on monomials is well-ordered (no infinite descending chains).
Hence, after finitely many steps we reach $G_k = 0$.

At that stage we have constructed symmetric polynomials $f_1, f_2, ..., f_{k-1}$ each of which is a monomial in $\sigma_1, ..., \sigma_n$ s.t. $G= f + f_1 + f_2 + ... + f_{k-1}$.
Thus $G$ is a polynomial in the elementary symmetric polynomials.
If the coefficients of $G$ were integers, all the coefficients in this construction are obtained by subtraction of integer polynomials, hence remain integers. Therefore the resulting $F(\sigma_1, ..., \sigma_n)$ has integer coefficients.
