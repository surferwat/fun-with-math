Describe the field of complex numbers as a simple algebraic extension of the field of real numbers.

Consider the polynomial $x^2 +1 \in \mathbb{R}[x]$.
This polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb{R}$ since it has no real roots.
Let $i$ be a root of this polynomial, so that $i^2+1 = 0$ or $i^2 = -1$.
By the theorem on simple algebraic extensions (as in the excerpt), we can form the field $\mathbb{R}(i)$, which consists of all polynomials in $i$ with coefficients in $\mathbb{R}$. Because $i^2 = -1$, every element of $\mathbb{R}(i)$ can be written in the form $a+bi$ with $a,b \in \mathbb{R}$.
But this is exactly the usual description of the complex numbers. 
Therefore $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)$.

Notes
- The process to select a polynomial involves 1) pick a non-real element that can generate $\mathbb{C} \to i$, 2) find its minimal polynomial over $\mathbb{R} \to x^2 + 1$, 3) verify irreducibility $\to$ no real roots, 4) conclude $\mathbb{C} = \mathbb{R}(i)$.
- Why $i$? It is the simplest non-real complex number to try since every complex number already has the form $a+bi (a,b \in \mathbb{R})$, so if you adjoin $i$, you automatically get all of $\mathbb{C}$. Thus, $i$ is a natural candidate for a generator.
- A minimal polynomial of an element $t$ over a field $K$ is defined as: 1) the monic polynomial in $K[x]$, 2) of smallest degree, and 3) such that $f(t)=0$. The minimal polynomial is always irreducible over $K$.

$\S33$ The basic use of the Galois resolvent is the following.
Let $K$ denote the set of all quantities that are to be regarded as known.
$K$ must include at least the rational numbers and the coefficients of the given equation $f(x) = 0$ but it may include more - for example, there may be some reason to regard certain roots of unity as known. Since sums, differences, products, and quotients (with nonzero denominator) of known quantities are known, $K$ is what is called a "field." (A field is a set with two operations - addition and multiplication - in which the familiar associative, commutative, and distributive laws hold, and in which substraction and division - determination of $x$ by $a+x=b$ or by $cx = d$ when $c \ne 0$ - are possible.)

Let $K(a,b,c,...)$ denote the set of all elements which can be expressed as polynomials in the roots $a,b,c$ ... of the given equation $f(x) = 0$ with coefficients in $K$. (The precise domain in which $a,b,c$ ...) and hence the elements of $K(a,b,c, ...)$ have their existence will be described later. At first, it is probably easist to think of $f(x)$ as having rational coefficients, of $K$ as being the rational numbers, and of $a,b,c$ ... as being complex numbers. Ultimately, however, it will be important to be able to think of the coefficients of $f(x)$ as being literal, that is, as being elements of $K$ that are transcendental over the rationals. All that matters is that there be some field $K(a,b,c, ...)$ containing $K$ and containing a complete set of roots $a,b,c$... of $f(x) = 0$. Such a field is called a splitting field of $f$.)

In particular, $t$ is in $K(a,b,c, ...)$. Let $K(t)$ denote the set of all elements of $K(a,b,c, ...)$ which can be expressed rationally in terms of $t$ with coefficients in $K$. Then the corollary of $\S31$ can be expressed by the equation $K(a,b,c,...) = K(t)$, that is, everything that can be expressed as a polynomial in the roots $a,b,c,...$ can be expressed rationally in terms of $t$ and vice versa. (It is not obvious that a rational function of $t$ can necessarily be expressed as a polynomial in $a,b,c,...,$ only that it can be expressed as a rational function in them. However, as is noted at the end of $\S36$, every rational function in $a,b,c, ...$ can be expressed as a polynomial in $a,b,c,...$.) This can be regarded as a solution of the equation $f(x) = 0$ because, as will be shown in the next three sections, $K(t)$ can be described in a very explicit way, and it is a field.Thus, the construction of $K(t)$ which follows is a construction of a field $K(t) = K(a,b,c,...)$ which contains roots $a,b,c,...$ of the given equation. 

(Strictly speaking, the construction of $K(t)$ which follows assumes that there is some field in which $f(x)=0$ has $n$ roots, so that, for example, $K(a,b,c,...)$ makes sense and provides a setting in which the computation of the polynomial $F(X) = \prod_S (X -St)$ can be carried out. In other words, it constructs a field containing roots assuming there exists a field containing roots. This assumption, which Girard, Newton, Lagrange, Galois, et al. seem to have made without question, will be justified in $\S49-61$).


$\S34$ Construction of the Field K(t)
Let $f(x)=0$ be an equation of degree $n$ with $n$ distinct roots $a,b,c,...$ and let $t$ be a Galois resolvent of this equation. Then $K(a,b,c,...) = K(t)$. The objective is to describe the field $K(t)$ as explicitly as possibly. By assumption, $t$ is a polynomial in $a,b,c,...$ which has the property that the $n!$ elements $St$ of $K(a,b,c,...)$ obtained by substituting the roots $a,b,c,...$ into $t$ in each of their $n!$ possible orders are distinct elements fo $K(a,b,c,...)$ (One can, in fact, assume that $t$ is a linear polynomial with integer coefficients.) The "equation for $t$," as in $\S29$, is the polynomial* $F(X) = \prod_S(X-St)$, where the product is over all $n!$ permutations $S$ of the roots. The coefficients of $F(X)$ are, in the first instance, in $K(a,b,c,...)$ but since they are symmetric polynomials in $a,b,c,...$ they can, by the fundamental theorem on symmetric polynomials in $a,b,c,...$ and therefore in terms of the coefficients of the given equation $f(x)=0$. Thus, $F(X)$ is a polynomial with known coefficients in $K$.

(The computation of F(X) is stupendously long in all but the simplest cases, but in principle it can be carried out. Given the integers, $A,B,C$,... which describe $t=Aa+Bb+Cc+...$, the coefficients of $F(X)$ are explicit symmetric polynomials in $a,b,c$,... with integer coefficients of $F(X)$ are explicit symmetric polynomials in $a,b,c$... with integer coefficients. Express them as polynomials in the elementary symmetric polynomials $\sigma_1$, $\sigma_2$, ...$\sigma_n$ in $a,b,c$... If the given equation is $B_0x^n + B_1x^{n-1} + ... + B_n = 0$ then $\sigma_i = (-1)^i B_i/B_0$ for $i = 1,2,...n$ that is, $f(x) = B_0(x-a)(x-b)(x-c)...$.  This describes each $\sigma_i$ as an element of $K$, and substitution of these values in the symmetric polynomials gives the coefficients of $F(X)$ as elements of $K$.)

The polynomial $F(X)$ with coefficients in $K$ has degree $n!$ and leading coefficient $1$. Let $F(X) = G_1(X)G_2(X) ... G_k(X)$ be its factorization into irreducible polynomials $G_1(X)$, $G_2(X)$, ..., $G_k(X)$ with coefficients in $K$ and leading coefficient $1$. (A polynomial $G(X)$ with coefficients in $K$ is said to be irreducible if there do not exist two polynomials $g(X)$, $h(X)$ with coefficients in $K$ such that $G(X) = g(X)h(X)$ and such that $g$ and $h$ are of lower degree than $G$. If one accepts the "obvious fact" that a polynomial is either irreducible or there is a factorization of it into two polynomials of lower degree then it is obvious that such a factorization of $F$ always exists. For a full discussion of the problem of factorization see $\S49-61$.) The substitution of $t$ for $X$ in $F(X)$ gives on the one hand, $F(t) = 0$ because $F(t)$ can be written as a product $\prod_S (t-St)$ in which one of the factors is $0$, and gives, on the other hand $F(t) = G_1(t)G_2(t)...G_k(t)$. Therefore $G_1(t)G_2(t) ... G_k(t) = 0$ and at least one of the factors $G_i(t)$ must be* $0$. In other words, the irreducible factors $G_1, G_2, ..., G_k$ of $F$ must include an irreducible polynomial of which $t$ is a root. The desired description of $K(t)$ is then given by the following basic theorem:

Theorem (Simple Algebraic Extension). Let $K$ be a given field and let $G(X)$ be an irreducible polynomial with coefficients in $K$. Then one can construct a field $K(t)$ s.t. (1) $K(t)$ contains $K$, (2) $K(t)$ contains an element $t$ which is a root of $G$, that is, which satisfies $G(t) = 0$, and (3) every element of $K(t)$ can be expressed as a polynomial in $t$ with coefficients in $K$, that is, given any $x \in K(t)$ there are an integer $v$ and $elements$ $b_0$, $b_1$, ..., $b_v$ of $K$ such that $x = b_0 + b_1t + ... + b_vt^v$. Moreover, any two such fields $K(t)$ are the same in a natural sense that will be spelled out in $\S36$.
