Prove as a corollary to the proposition of $\S46$ that for any prime $p$ the $p$th roots of unity can be obtained by adjoining a succession of radicals to the rational field $\mathbb{Q}$.

Let $\zeta_p=e^{2\pi i/p}$ be a primitive $p$th root of unity. 
We wish to show that the field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ can be obtained from $\mathbb{Q}$ by adjoining a succession of radicals.

The polynomial $\Phi_p(x)=\frac{x^p-1}{x-1}=x^{p-1}+x^{p-2}+\cdots+x+1$ is the $p$th cyclotomic polynomial. 
By §42, its Galois group over any field of characteristic $0$ is a subgroup of the cyclic group of order $p-1$. 
Hence $G=\operatorname{Gal}(\Phi_p/K)$ is cyclic, and therefore solvable.

Since every finite cyclic group is solvable, there exists a chain of normal subgroups $G=G_0 \triangleright G_1 \triangleright \cdots \triangleright G_n=\{1\}$ such that each quotient $G_i/G_{i+1}$ has prime order. 
These prime orders are precisely the prime factors of $p-1$.

Now suppose the base field $K$ contains a primitive $q$th root of unity for every prime $q\mid(p-1)$.
Then each step in the above chain satisfies the hypotheses of the proposition of §46: the quotient has prime index $q$, and the required primitive $q$th root of unity already lies in the base field. 
Therefore, at each stage, the proposition shows that the corresponding field extension is obtained by adjoining a $q$th root of an element of the current field. 
Repeating this process for each subgroup in the chain expresses the splitting field of $\Phi_p(x)$, namely $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$, as a succession of radical extensions of $K$.

Thus $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ can be obtained by radicals over any field containing the necessary roots of unity of orders dividing $p-1$.

It remains only to remove this assumption on $K$. 
Let $p_1,\ldots,p_r$ be the distinct prime divisors of $p-1$. 
Since every $p_i < p$, proving the theorem for the prime $p$ reduces it to proving it for the smaller prime $p_i$.
Assume inductively that, for every prime $p_i < p$, the field $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p_i})$ can be obtained from $\mathbb{Q}$ by adjoining radicals.
Then taking $K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p_1},\ldots,\zeta_{p_r})$ shows that $K=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{p_1}, \dots, \zeta_{p_r})$ is a radical extension of $\mathbb{Q}$. 
Since $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ is a radical extension of $K$, the transitivity of radical extensions implies that $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_p)$ is a radical extension of $\mathbb{Q}$.

The induction begins with the case $p=2$, where the only primitive second root of unity is $-1 \in \mathbb{Q}$.
Therefore, by induction on the prime $p$, every primitive $p$th root of unity can be obtained from $\mathbb{Q}$ by adjoining a succession of radicals.

Author's solution
The Galois group of $(x^p-1)/(x-1)$ over any field (of characteristic $0$) is a subgroup of the cyclic group of order $p-1$ (see $\S42$). Therefore the Galois group is solvable. If $K$ contains $p$th roots of unity for all the indices that occur in this sequence of subgroups - which is guaranteed if it contains $p_i$th roots of unity for all prime factors $p_i$ of $p-1$ - then, by the proposition of $\S46$, all $p$th roots of unity for all prime factors $p_i$ of $p-1$ - then, by the proposition of $\S46$, all $p$th roots of unity can be obtained by adjoining radicals to $K$. Thus it suffices to show that all $p_i$th roots of unity can be obtained by radical adjunctions to $\mathbb{Q}$ where $p_i \backslash p-1$. An introduction now sets in. See $\S65$.

$\S46$ proposition
Let $G$ be the Galois group of $f(x)=0$ over $K$ and let $G'$ be a normal subgroup of $G$ of prime index $p$. Assume also that $K$ contains a primitive $p$th root of unity $\alpha$, that is, a solution of $\alpha^p=1$ with $\alpha \ne 1$. Then there is an element $k$ of $K$ such that if $K' \supset K$ is the field obtained by adjoining a $p$th root of $k$ of $K$ then the Galois group of $f(x)=0$ over $K'$ is $G'$.
