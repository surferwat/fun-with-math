As in $\S30$, let $t$ be a polynomial in $n$ variables $x_1$, $x_2$, ..., $x_n$, let $t_1$, $t_2$, ..., $t_k$ be all distinct polynomials in $x_1$, $x_2$, ..., $x_n$ that can be obtained from $t$ permutation of the variables, and let $\phi_1$, $\phi_2$, ..., $\phi_k$ be permutations of the variables such that $t_i = \phi_i t$. Show that if $\psi$ is any permutation of the variables $\psi = \phi_i g$ - that is, first perform the permutation $g$ and then perform the permutation $\phi_i$ - where $i=1$ or $2$ or ... or $k$ and where $gt=t$.

Let $t\in k[x_1, ..., x_n]$, and let $t_1$, $t_2$, ..., $t_k$ be all distinct polynomials obtained from $t$ by permuting the variables. 
For each $i$, choose a permutation $\phi_i \in S_n$ s.t. $t_i = \phi_i(t)$.
Let $G = \{ g \in S_n | g(t) = t \}$ be the subgroup of permutations that fix $t$.

**Goal**
Show that any permutation $\psi \in S_n$ can be written as $\psi = \phi_i \circ g$ for some $i \in \{1,...,k \}$, $g \in G$.

**Step 1: Apply $\psi$ to $t$**
Let $\psi \in S_n$. 
Then $\psi(t)$ is a polynomial obtained from $t$ by permuting variables.
By definition of the list $\{ t_1, ..., t_k \}$ there exists some $i$ s.t. $\psi(t) = t_i$.
But we also know $t_i = \phi_i(t)$, so $\psi(t) = \phi_i(t)$.

**Step 2: Compare permutations**
Rewriting: $\phi_i^{-1} \circ \psi(t) = t$.
Define: $g := \phi_i^{-1} \circ \psi$.
Then: $g(t) = t$, so $g \in G$.

**Step 3: Rearranging**
From the definition of $g$, we get: $\psi = \phi_i \circ g$.

**Conclusion**
Every permutation $\psi \in S_n$ can be written in the form $\psi = \phi_i \circ g$ with $g(t) =t$.
This proves the claim.

Notes
- $\S29$ Lagrange's theorem essentially saying: if $t$ captures all the "symmetry distinctions" that $y$ depends on, then $y$ can be algebraically recovered from $t$.
- The key idea is recognizing that the orbit of $t$ under $S_n$ is exactly $\{ t_1, ..., t_k \}$. The subgroup $G$ is the stabilizer of $t$. The proof is essentially showing: $S_n = \bigcup_{i=1}^k \phi_i G$ (i.e., a decomposition into cosets of the stabilizer).
- The proof shows that essentially every permutation can be decomposed into (a representative that changes the polynomial) + (a symmetry that leaves it unchanged). In modern language, this is: a decomposition of the group into cosets of the stabilizer and a concrete instance of the orbit-stabilizer principle.
- The proof shows the separation of "real change" from "fake change". When a permutation acts on $t$: some permutations genuinely produce a new polynomial $t_i$ while others only rearrange variables but don't change $t$. This result tells you: every transformation can be broken into: a part that actually changes $t$ and a part that doesn't matter for $t$. This separation is fundamental in Galois theory.
- This problem is a prototype of a major theorem in that it is essentially a concrete version of: group actions, cosets, orbit-stabilizer theorem but framed in terms of polynomials and permutations, which is exactly the language of Galois theory.

In $\S29$ he states "in order to see the situation in which the "generally speaking" provision is necessary, let $t$ be a polynomial in the roots $x', x'', x'''$, ... of the given equation with coefficients that are known quantities. If $\phi$ is any permutation of $x'$, $x''$, $x'''$, ... let $\phi t$ be the polynomial in $x'$, $x''$, $x'''$, ... obtained by applying $\phi$ to the x's in $t$. It may be that $\phi t = t$ even when $\phi \ne$ identity. (For example, when $n=4$ and $t=x' + x'' - x''' - x^{(iv)}$ as in $\S17$, the twenty-four permutations of the $x$'s produce only six different polynomials $\phi t$.) The "equation for $t$" is the polynomial $F(X) = (X-t)(X-\phi_1 t)(X-\phi_2t)...(X-\phi_k t)$, where $t$, $\phi_1 t$, $\phi_2 t$ ... is a list of all distinct polynomials that can be obtained from $t$, $\phi_1 t$, $\phi_2 t$, is a list of all distinct polynomials that can be obtained from $t$ by permutations of the $x$'s. The coefficients of $F(X)$ are symmetric polynomials in $t$, $\phi_1 t$, $\phi_2 t$, ..., and since a permutation of $x'$, $x''$, $x'''$, ... merely permutes $t$, $\phi_1 t$, $\phi_2 t$, ...  they are therefore symmetric polynomials in $x'$, $x''$, $x'''$, ... which means that the coefficients of $F(X)$ are known quantities. Thus the polynomial $F(X)$ is known polynomial of which $t$ is a root. Lagrange says that if $t$ is a simple root of $F(X)$ and if $\phi t = t$ implies $\phi y = y$ then $y$ can be expressed rationally in terms of $t$ and known quantities. If $t$ is a multiple root of $F(X)$ (since $F(X) = \prod (X- \phi_i t)$ this means that one of the polynomials $\phi_i t$ has, although it is formally distinct from $t$, the same numerical value when numerical values of the roots $x'$, $x''$, $x'''$, ... are used) then $y$ is not a rational function of $t$, but is a root of a polynomial whose coefficients are rational functions of $t$ and whose degree is equal to the multiplicity of $t$ as a root of $F(X)$. For sake of simplicity, Lagrange's theorem will be proved only in the case where $F(X)$ has only simple roots, that is, where the formally distinct polynomials $t$, $\phi_1 t$, $\phi_2 t$,... are all numerically distinct. This is the only case that is necessary for Galois theory.

Proof of Lagrange's Theorem in the case where the equation for $t$ has simple roots. Let $t$ and $y$ be given polynomials in the $n$ roots, and let $t_1$, $t_2$, ... $t_k$ be all the distinct polynomials in the roots that can be obtained from $t$ by permutation of the roots. By assumption, any permutation which leaves $t$ unchanged leaves $y$ unchanged. Therefore, there are at most $k$ different polynomials that can be obtained from $y$ by permuting the variables, and there are polynomials $y_1$, $y_2$, ... $y_k$ such that the permutations which carry $t$ to $t_i$ carry $y$ to $y_i$. (Explicitly, let $\phi_1$, $\phi_2$, ..., $\phi_k$ be permutations such that $t_i = \phi_i t$ and set $y_i = \phi_i y$. Every permutation $\psi$ can be written in the form $\psi = \phi_i g$ where $i=1$ or $2$ or ... or $k$ and where $g$ is a permutation which leaves $t$ unchanged. Then $gy = y$ and $\psi y=\phi_i y = y_i$, so the list $y_1, y_2, ...$ includes all polynomials of the form $\psi y$.)

The key to Lagrange's theorem is to consider the $k$ polynomials 
$y_1 + y_2 + ... + y_k$
$t_1 y_1 + t_2 y_2 + ... + t_k y_k$
$t_1^2 y_1 + t_2^2 y_2 + ... + t_k^2 y_k$
...
$t_1^{k-1} y_1 + t_2^{k-1} y_2 + ... + t_k^{k-1} y_k$.
These polynomials are symmetric (a permutation of $x', x'', x''', ...$ merely permutes the terms of each sum) and therefore their numerical values are known (expressible in terms of the coefficients of $t$ and $y$ and in terms of the coefficients $m,n,p$.. of the given equation). What is to be shown is that $y_1$ can be expressed rationally in terms of $t_1$ and known quantities. For this, it suffices to apply Cramer's rule. (Lagrange's approach is more inclusive because he does not make the simplifying assumption that $F(X)$ has simple roots)

Cramer's rule applied to the equations $\sum t_i^j y_i = c_j$, where $c_j$ is a known quantity, gives $y_{\mu} = D_{\mu}/\Delta$ where $\Delta$ is the Vandermonde determinant $det(t_i^j) = \prod_{k<i} (t_i - t_k)$ and where $D_{\mu}$ is the determinant obtained by replacing the $\mu$th column of $\Delta$ by the column of known quantities $c_j$. The square of the denominator $\Delta^2 = \prod_{k<i} (t_i -t_k)^2$ is simply the discriminant of $F(X) = \prod (X - t_i)$; by assumption this is not $0$, and of course it is a known quantity. It suffices, then, to show that the numerator of $y_1 = D_1 \Delta / \Delta^2$ can be expressed rationally in terms of $t_1$. In fact, it is easy to see that $D_1 \Delta$ is a polynomial in $t_1$. On its face, it is a polynomial in all the $t_i$ (and in the known quantities $c_i$). However, permutation of two of the $t_i$ other than $t_1$ changes the sign of both $D_1$ and $\Delta$, and therefore leaves $D_1 \Delta$ unchanged. Thus, $D_1 \Delta$ can be expressed as a polynomial in $t_1$ whose coefficients are symmetric polynomials in $t_2$, $t_3$, ... $t_k$. These coefficients can, by the fundamental theorem on symmetric functions, be expressed in terms of the elementary symmetric functions in $k-1$ variables $\tau_1 = t_2 + t_3 + ...$, $\tau_2 = t_2 t_3 + t_2 t_4 + ...$, $\tau_3 = \sigma_3 - t_1 \sigma_2 + t_1^2 \sigma_1 - t_1^3$, ..., where the $\sigma$'s being symmetric functions of the $t$'s are known quantities. Thus the $\tau$'s and consequently $D_1 \Delta$ can be expressed in terms of $t_1$ and known quantities, as was to be shown.
