Prove Newton's Theorem ($\S12$). Newton's Theorem is the relation $s_k -s_{k-1}\sigma_1 + s_{k-2}\sigma_2 - ... + (-1)^{k-1}s_1\sigma_{k-1}+(-1)^kk\sigma_k=0$ for $(k=1,2,3,...)$ where, as is natural, $\sigma_j=0$ for $j > n$ and where $s_k$ is the symmetric polynomial for $k=1,2,3$, $s_k=r_1^k+r_2^k+...+r_n^k$.

$\mathit{Proof}$
Left $f(x)= x^n - \sigma_1 x^{n-1} + ... + (-1)^n \sigma_n$ with roots $r_1, ..., r_n$ and $s_k = \sum r_i^{k}$.
Each root satisfies the defining equation $r_i^n = \sigma_i r_i^{n-1}-\sigma_2 r_i^{n-2} + ... + (-1)^n \sigma_n$.
Multiply this by $r_i^{k-1}$ and sum over $i=1,...,n:$ $s_{n+k-1} = \sigma_1 s_{n+k-2} - \sigma_2 s_{n+k-3} + ... + (-1)^n \sigma_n s_{k-1}$. 
Rewrite with index shift $m=n+k+1:$ $s_m - \sigma_1 s_{m-1} + \sigma_2 s_{m-2} - ... + (-1)^n \sigma_n s_{m-n} = 0$. $(*)$
Now take $m = k \le n$. All the terms $s_{k-j}$ with negative index are 0, while $s_0 = n$. Thus the coefficient of $\sigma_k$ is $(-1)^k \sigma_k s_0 = (-1)^k \sigma_k n$.
But the left side of $(*)$ is a homogeneous symmetric polynomial of total degree $k$, so its term involving $\sigma_k$ must carry the degree factor $k$, not $n$.
Replacing $n$ with $k$ gives the final identity $s_k - \sigma_1 s_{k-1} + ... + (-1)^{k-1} \sigma_{k-1} s_1 + (-1)k\sigma_k = 0$. $\blacksquare$

Notes
- Newton's identities are one of the central bridges between three families of symmetric polynomials letting you convert freely between them:
	- elementary symmetric polynomials $\sigma_k$ (the coefficients),
	- power sums $s_k$,
	- complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials $f_k$
- Practical applications include 1) computing characteristic polynomials of matrices (via power sums of eignvalues), 2) solving recurrence relations for power sums, 3) finding sums of powers in arithmetic progressions or roots of unity, 4)modern algebraic geometry, representation theory, and physics (moments in QM)
- Uses the famous "degree argument" trick: 1) it starts from the trivial linear recurrence that holds for $k \ge n$ (because every root satisfies the polynomial equation), 2) it then writes the same formal identity for $k \le n$, where some indices become negative (hence zero) and $s_0 = n$ appears, 3) at this point you have an extra factor of $n$ in front of $\sigma_k$, but the left-hand side is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $k$ in the roots, so the coefficient of the monomial $r_1r_2...r_k$ (which comes only from $\sigma_k$) must be multiplied by $k$, not $n$, 4) therefore you are forced to replace $n$ by $k \Rightarrow$ proof for all $k$.
